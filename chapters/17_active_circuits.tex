\chapter{Active Circuits, Feedback, and Oscillators}
\label{ch:active}
Everything so far has been \emph{passive}: circuits that store and dissipate the
energy handed to them. An active circuit adds a device that can supply gain, and with
gain comes the one operation passive circuits cannot perform---feedback around a
loop. This chapter is therefore where \cref{ch:feedback} finally meets hardware, and
where the resonators of \cref{ch:rlc,ch:rlcpar} stop being things to analyze and
become things a designer places deliberately.

\section{Why Active Circuits Appear}
The Extra exam includes amplifiers, op-amps, feedback, oscillators, mixers, and
regulated power supplies. A full electronics text is not needed here; the goal is
to connect the circuit formulas to system behavior---and, in this chapter, to the
classical feedback ideas that a controls background already contains. The device
details (bias points, transistor models, tube parameters) live in the ARRL
manual; what follows is the systems view that ties them together. The feedback
groundwork of \cref{ch:feedback}---closed-loop gain \(A/(1+A\beta)\), the
large-loop-gain limit \(A_{\text{cl}}\to1/\beta\), pole motion under feedback, and
the gain and phase margins---is the backbone of this chapter. Rather than restate
those results, this chapter puts them to work on real devices, so it is worth having
\cref{ch:feedback} fresh in mind.

\section{Amplifier Gain and Classes}
\label{sec:classes}
Voltage gain is \(A_v=V_o/V_i\); in decibels \(A_{v,\dB}=20\log_{10}|A_v|\), and power
gain is \(G_{\dB}=10\log_{10}(P_o/P_i)\)---the \(20\log\) versus \(10\log\)
distinction of \cref{sec:decibels}.

Amplifier \emph{class} describes how much of the input cycle the active device
conducts, which trades linearity against efficiency:
\begin{description}
\item[Class A] conducts the full \(360^\circ\); most linear, least efficient.
\item[Class B / AB] Class B conducts half the cycle (\(180^\circ\)); Class AB
somewhat more than half. Higher efficiency than Class A, with some crossover
distortion; common in linear single-sideband (SSB) power amplifiers.
\item[Class C] conducts less than half the cycle; efficient but nonlinear, used
where a tuned output tank restores the sinusoid (as in frequency-modulation, FM,
and continuous-wave Morse, CW, transmitters).
\end{description}
Class C is nonlinear on purpose: the output tank is the high-\(Q\) parallel resonator
of \cref{ch:rlcpar} (a pole pair near the \(\jj\omega\) axis) that rings at the
fundamental and rejects the harmonics the clipped current injects. The same nonlinearity that spoils
linearity is what makes mixers and frequency multipliers work.

\subsection{Where the Efficiency Numbers Come From}
\label{sec:conductionangle}
The efficiencies usually quoted alongside those classes---\SI{50}{\percent},
\SI{25}{\percent}, \SI{78.5}{\percent}---are not conventions to memorize. They are one
integral evaluated at three conduction angles, and the tool is
\cref{sec:fourier}'s: a periodic current \emph{is} its harmonics, so ask which
harmonic each part of the circuit is paid for.

Let the device current be a cosine truncated at half-angle \(\theta\), so the
conduction angle is \(2\theta\):
\[
i(\phi)=I_{pk}\,\frac{\cos\phi-\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta},
\qquad |\phi|\le\theta,
\]
and zero elsewhere. Only two of its Fourier coefficients matter. The supply delivers
the DC component, and the tuned load is paid only at the fundamental
(\cref{ch:rlcpar} rejects everything else), so
\[
I_{\mathrm{dc}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}\!i\,\dd\phi
=I_{pk}\,\frac{\sin\theta-\theta\cos\theta}{\pi\,(1-\cos\theta)},
\qquad
I_1=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}\!i\cos\phi\,\dd\phi
=I_{pk}\,\frac{\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\pi\,(1-\cos\theta)} .
\]
Both integrals are elementary---the second needs only
\(\int\cos^2=\tfrac12(\phi+\sin\phi\cos\phi)\). Now the efficiency is output power over
input power. The load gets \(\tfrac12V_1I_1\) at the fundamental; the supply pays
\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}I_{\mathrm{dc}}\). Hence
\[
\boxed{\;\eta=\frac{\tfrac12V_1I_1}{V_{\mathrm{dc}}I_{\mathrm{dc}}}
=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{I_1}{I_{\mathrm{dc}}}\,\frac{V_1}{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}\;}
\]
Two factors, and they are independent: a \emph{current} ratio set purely by the
conduction angle, and a \emph{voltage} ratio set purely by how the load is coupled.

Take the voltage factor first, because it is where the two Class A numbers part
company. A transformer or a tuned tank places no DC across itself, so the output can
swing a full \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}\) either side of the supply rail and
\(V_1/V_{\mathrm{dc}}\to1\). A resistive load, by contrast, must carry the DC current
too, dropping half the supply before any signal appears---so
\(V_1/V_{\mathrm{dc}}\le\tfrac12\) and the efficiency is halved. That is the entire
content of ``\SI{50}{\percent} transformer-coupled, \SI{25}{\percent} resistively
coupled.'' Nothing about the device changed.

Now the current factor, which \cref{fig:conduction-angle} shows alongside the
waveforms that produce it. Evaluating the two coefficients:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccl@{}}
\toprule
Conduction angle \(2\theta\) & Class & \(I_1/I_{\mathrm{dc}}\) & \(\eta\) (tuned load) \\
\midrule
\(360^\circ\) & A  & \(1\)            & \(\tfrac12=\SI{50.0}{\percent}\) \\
\(240^\circ\) & AB & \(1.321\)        & \SI{66.1}{\percent} \\
\(180^\circ\) & B  & \(\pi/2\)        & \(\pi/4=\SI{78.5}{\percent}\) \\
\(120^\circ\) & C  & \(1.794\)        & \SI{89.7}{\percent} \\
\(\to0^\circ\) & C & \(\to2\)         & \(\to\SI{100}{\percent}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two exact entries are worth doing by hand. At \(\theta=\pi\) both coefficients
collapse to \(I_{pk}/2\), so \(\eta=\tfrac12\) exactly. At \(\theta=\pi/2\),
\(\cos\theta=0\) kills a term in each, leaving \(I_{\mathrm{dc}}=I_{pk}/\pi\) and
\(I_1=I_{pk}/2\), so
\(\eta=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}=\pi/4\). The famous \SI{78.5}{\percent} is
\(\pi/4\), and it is \(\pi/4\) because a half-wave rectified cosine has exactly those
two components.

Read the last row as the honest statement of the linearity--efficiency trade: narrowing
the conduction angle drives \(\eta\) toward \SI{100}{\percent}, but \(I_1\) itself is
shrinking, so the \emph{power} goes to zero along with the loss. Class C buys
efficiency with output power and with linearity, which is why it is confined to
signals whose amplitude carries no information.

\subsection{Why Switching Amplifiers Escape the Table}
\label{sec:switching}
A Class D or E amplifier is not further along that curve---it is off it, and the
argument takes one line. Instantaneous dissipation in the device is
\(p(t)=v(t)\,i(t)\). An ideal switch is either closed, with \(v=0\), or open, with
\(i=0\). Either way \emph{one factor is zero}, so \(p(t)=0\) at every instant and
\[
\int_0^T p(t)\,\dd t=0
\]
identically---no integral to evaluate, no conduction angle, no dependence on the
waveform at all. There is no efficiency ceiling to derive because the ideal device
dissipates nothing.

What is left is a list of the ways a real switch fails to be one, and each names a
real design constraint: finite on-resistance costs \(i^2R_{\mathrm{on}}\) while
conducting; finite switching time means both \(v\) and \(i\) are briefly nonzero
during each transition, so loss grows with switching frequency; and the output is a
\emph{square} wave, whose harmonics (\cref{sec:fourier}) are entirely
unwanted. Hence the one thing a switching amplifier cannot do without: a low-pass or
band-pass filter on its output, which is the \cref{ch:filters} design problem.

\subsection{Push-Pull: Even Orders Cancel Identically}
\label{sec:pushpull}
A push-pull stage drives two devices in antiphase and subtracts their outputs. Write
the device's input--output relation as a power series---any smooth nonlinearity has
one, and \cref{sec:powerseries} will make heavy use of the same expansion:
\[
f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n .
\]
One device sees \(x\), the other \(-x\), and the output is the difference. Since
\((-x)^n=(-1)^nx^n\),
\[
f(x)-f(-x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\left[1-(-1)^n\right]x^n
=2\!\!\sum_{n\ \mathrm{odd}}\!\!a_nx^n .
\]
The bracket is \(0\) for even \(n\) and \(2\) for odd \(n\). \emph{Every even-order
term vanishes, for any \(f\) whatsoever}---the \(a_n\) never had to be known, let
alone controlled. Two consequences follow that are easy to get wrong.

\textbf{The pair must be matched, not specially biased.} Nothing above referenced the
operating point, so no bias trick is involved; a push-pull Class A, AB, B or C stage
cancels even orders equally. What the derivation \emph{did} assume is that both
devices obey the \emph{same} \(f\). Give them different series \(f\) and \(g\) and the
output is \(\sum(a_n-(-1)^nb_n)x^n\), whose even terms are \(a_n-b_n\)---zero only to
the extent that the devices match. That is why push-pull power amplifiers are built
from matched pairs, and why a drifting or mismatched pair shows up first as a
\emph{second} harmonic.

\textbf{It does nothing whatever for third-order intermodulation.} The troublesome
products of \cref{sec:mixers}---\(2f_1-f_2\) and \(2f_2-f_1\)---come from the cubic
term, and cubic is odd. Push-pull doubles the odd terms, so it doubles the wanted
fundamental \emph{and} the third-order products by exactly the same factor, leaving
their ratio untouched. A push-pull final is therefore cleaner in even harmonics and no
better at all on the intermodulation figures that
\cref{sec:powerseries,sec:dynamicrange} show actually set receiver and transmitter
dynamic range. It is a filter you get for free in \emph{order}, not in \emph{level}.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{Symmetry, not cancellation of quantities.} It is tempting to describe this as
two distortions cancelling because they are equal and opposite, but the derivation
says something stronger and cheaper: the antiphase-and-subtract operation extracts the
\emph{odd part} of \(f\), and the even part is discarded before it can appear.
Decomposing a response into even and odd parts is the same style of move as splitting
a signal into symmetric and antisymmetric components, or a transmission line's
\(V\) and \(I\) into forward and reverse waves (\cref{ch:lines})---choose the
decomposition the circuit's own symmetry prefers, and the unwanted half is not
suppressed but absent.
\end{controlsbox}

The same algebra explains a mixer, and it is worth spending three more lines here so
that \cref{sec:mixers} can lean on the result instead of asserting it. Apply the
antiphase-and-subtract trick to the \emph{RF} port while feeding the local oscillator
to both devices in common:
\[
y=f(v_{\mathrm{LO}}+v_{\mathrm{RF}})-f(v_{\mathrm{LO}}-v_{\mathrm{RF}}) .
\]
This is the odd part with respect to \(v_{\mathrm{RF}}\), so every term that does
\emph{not} contain an odd power of \(v_{\mathrm{RF}}\) is gone---and that includes
every term in \(v_{\mathrm{LO}}\) alone. Set \(v_{\mathrm{RF}}=0\) and the output is
identically zero: the oscillator, however strong, leaks nothing. Meanwhile the
quadratic term contributes \(a_2\left[(v_{\mathrm{LO}}+v_{\mathrm{RF}})^2-(v_{\mathrm{LO}}-v_{\mathrm{RF}})^2\right]=4a_2v_{\mathrm{LO}}v_{\mathrm{RF}}\),
the wanted product, doubled. \emph{Carrier suppression in a balanced modulator is not
a trimming adjustment; it is the same parity argument as push-pull, applied to a
different port}---and the residual carrier a real one leaks is, once again, a measure
of how well its two halves match.

\begin{exambox}
The pool's amplifier-class questions (group E7B) ask for the mechanism, not the
percentage. A Class C stage on SSB gives ``distortion and excessive bandwidth,''
because amplitude \emph{is} the information in SSB and Class C does not preserve it.
Switching amplifiers are efficient because ``the switching device is at saturation or
cutoff most of the time''---which is \(v=0\) or \(i=0\), the one-line argument above.
And a switching amplifier requires ``a filter to remove harmonic content'' at its
output, for the reason just given. The derivation is not needed to answer these, but
it is what makes all three the same fact.
\end{exambox}

\section{Op-Amp Ideals}
An ideal op-amp has very high input impedance, very low output impedance, and
very large open-loop gain \(A(s)\). Of those three, only the last is an independent
assumption---\cref{sec:feedbackz} derives the other two from it.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{How the op-amp is modeled.} An op-amp is \emph{not} a reactive element
like an inductor or capacitor---it stores no energy and contributes no pole of its
own in the ideal picture. It is modeled as a \textbf{dependent source}:
specifically a voltage-controlled voltage source (VCVS) whose output is
\(A(s)\,(v_+-v_-)\), the open-loop gain times the difference of the two input
voltages. Only \emph{one} idealization is really attached to that source: that
\(A(s)\) is enormous (and rolls off with frequency, \cref{sec:gbw}). The other two
usually listed alongside it---infinite input impedance and zero output
impedance---need not be assumed at all. Give the model a finite \(R_{id}\) across its
inputs and a finite \(R_o\) in series with its output, close a loop of gain \(L\)
around it, and \cref{sec:feedbackz} shows the input impedance comes out
\(R_{id}(1+L)\) and the output impedance \(R_o/(1+L)\). The ideals are the
\(L\to\infty\) limit of that, not axioms. Every op-amp result below is bookkeeping on
one dependent source plus the feedback wired around it.
\end{physicalbox}

Negative feedback makes practical closed-loop gains predictable:
\[
\text{inverting:}\quad \frac{V_o}{V_i}=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}},
\qquad
\text{non-inverting:}\quad \frac{V_o}{V_i}=1+\frac{R_f}{R_g}.
\]
These come from the two ideal rules---no current into the inputs, and the inputs
driven to equal voltage by feedback---but both rules are consequences of a deeper
statement: the loop gain is large. The inverting configuration
(\cref{fig:opamp-inv}) makes this concrete: with the non-inverting input grounded
and the inverting input held at a virtual ground by feedback, the input current
\(V_i/R_{in}\) must flow on through \(R_f\), giving \(V_o=-R_f\,(V_i/R_{in})\).

\subsection{Where \texorpdfstring{\(\beta\)}{beta} Is Hiding}
\label{sec:beta-resistors}
\Cref{ch:feedback} worked entirely with an abstract feedback fraction \(\beta\), and
it is worth connecting that symbol to actual components before going further---because
every result in that chapter then becomes an op-amp result.

Look at the non-inverting amplifier. The output drives the divider \(R_f\)--\(R_g\),
and the voltage fed back to the inverting input is what that divider delivers:
\[
v_-=V_o\,\frac{R_g}{R_f+R_g}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;\beta=\frac{R_g}{R_f+R_g}\;}
\]
So \(\beta\) is nothing more exotic than \cref{ch:resistive}'s voltage divider,
running backwards from output to input. The large-loop-gain limit
\(A_{\mathrm{cl}}\to1/\beta\) of \cref{ch:feedback} then reads
\[
\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{R_f+R_g}{R_g}=1+\frac{R_f}{R_g},
\]
which is exactly the non-inverting gain formula above. The textbook result and the
control-theory result are the same statement.

Keeping \(A\) finite shows what the ideal rules cost. Substituting into
\(A_{\mathrm{cl}}=A/(1+A\beta)\) and factoring out \(1/\beta\),
\[
A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\frac{A\beta}{1+A\beta}
=\underbrace{\Bigl(1+\frac{R_f}{R_g}\Bigr)}_{\text{ideal}}
\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+1/L}}_{\text{error}},
\qquad L=A\beta ,
\]
so the fractional gain error is about \(1/L\)---\cref{ch:feedback}'s sensitivity
result, in the form a designer uses. With \(A=10^5\) and a gain of \(100\)
(\(\beta=10^{-2}\), so \(L=1000\)) the error is \(0.1\%\), which is why the ideal
formula is safe.

The same substitution quantifies the virtual ground. The op-amp's differential input
voltage is not zero but
\[
v_+-v_-=\frac{V_o}{A},
\]
so for \(V_o=\SI{10}{\volt}\) and \(A=10^5\) the two inputs differ by
\SI{100}{\micro\volt}. ``The inputs are at the same voltage'' is not an axiom---it is
a consequence of \(A\) being enormous, and it degrades exactly as \(A\) falls with
frequency.

\subsection{Feedback Sets Impedance}
\label{sec:feedbackz}
The other two ideals are consequences too, and the same \(1+L\) does both. Neither
derivation needs more than a test source and \cref{ch:resistive}'s Th\'evenin
thinking.

\textbf{Output impedance is divided by \(1+L\).} Give the op-amp a realistic output:
an internal source \(A\,v_d\) behind a resistance \(R_o\). Ground the input, apply a
test voltage \(V_t\) at the output, and ask what current it takes. The feedback
divider samples the output, so \(v_-=\beta V_t\) while \(v_+=0\), making
\(v_d=-\beta V_t\) and the internal source \(A v_d=-A\beta V_t\). The test source
therefore faces not \(V_t\) across \(R_o\) but \(V_t\) \emph{plus} that opposing
source:
\[
I_t=\frac{V_t-Av_d}{R_o}=\frac{V_t\left(1+A\beta\right)}{R_o}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;Z_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R_o}{1+L}\;}
\]
The mechanism is worth saying in words: try to drag the output away from where
feedback wants it, and the loop sees the error and pushes back. The harder you pull,
the harder it pushes---which is precisely what a low output impedance \emph{is}.

\textbf{Input impedance is multiplied by \(1+L\).} Now give the op-amp a finite
differential input resistance \(R_{id}\) and drive the non-inverting input with
\(V_t\). The voltage across \(R_{id}\) is \(v_d=V_t-\beta V_o\) and \(V_o=Av_d\), so
\(v_d\left(1+A\beta\right)=V_t\) and the differential voltage is squeezed down by the
loop:
\[
I_t=\frac{v_d}{R_{id}}=\frac{V_t}{R_{id}\left(1+L\right)}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;Z_{\mathrm{in}}=R_{id}\left(1+L\right)\;}
\]
Again the mechanism is the point: feedback works to make \(v_d\) vanish, and a
resistor with no voltage across it draws no current. The input looks like an open
circuit not because the transistors are perfect but because the loop is refusing to
let a voltage develop.

So ``infinite input impedance'' and ``zero output impedance'' are the \(L\to\infty\)
limit of two ordinary circuit results. A \SI{1}{\mega\ohm} input and a
\SI{75}{\ohm} output, wrapped in a loop of \(L=10^5\), become
\SI{100}{\giga\ohm} and \SI{750}{\micro\ohm}---not infinite and not zero, but far
enough past the resistors around them that no measurement in the circuit can tell.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{One factor, five consequences.} Everything feedback buys is the same number.
\Cref{ch:feedback} showed the loop divides gain by \(1+L\) and divides the plant's
fractional errors by \(1+L\); \cref{sec:gbw} below shows it multiplies bandwidth by
\(1+L\); and the two results above divide output impedance and multiply input
impedance by \(1+L\). Distortion, being a gain that varies with signal level, is
divided by it as well. Surplus gain is the currency and \(1+L\) is the exchange
rate---which is also why \cref{ch:feedback}'s stability question is unavoidable: the
only way to buy more of any of these is to raise \(L\), and \(1+L=0\) is where the
loop oscillates.
\end{controlsbox}

\begin{mistakebox}
\textbf{Feedback does not always \emph{raise} input impedance---it depends where the
loop samples and compares.} The result above is for the non-inverting connection,
where the feedback voltage is subtracted \emph{in series} with the input. The
inverting connection of \cref{fig:opamp-inv} compares \emph{currents} at a node
instead, and there the same loop action drives the input impedance \emph{down}: the
inverting terminal is held at a virtual ground, so what the source sees is just
\(R_{in}\), and the op-amp's own \(R_{id}\) is irrelevant. That is why an inverting
stage loads its source and a non-inverting one does not---and it is a property of the
\emph{topology}, not of the chip. The op-amp data sheet's ``input impedance'' is
\(R_{id}\), which is neither of these numbers.
\end{mistakebox}

\section{Feedback as a Loop}
Model the amplifier as a forward path \(A(s)\) with a fraction \(\beta\) of the
output fed back and subtracted at the input (\cref{fig:feedback-loop}). This is
\cref{ch:feedback}'s block diagram with the amplifier's names attached, so the
results below are that chapter's results read as hardware.

gain is \(L(s)=A(s)\beta\).}
\label{fig:feedback-loop}

The closed-loop gain is
\[
A_{\text{cl}}(s)=\frac{A(s)}{1+A(s)\beta}
=\frac{A(s)}{1+L(s)},
\qquad
L(s)=A(s)\beta .
\]
When the loop gain \(|L|\gg1\), \(A_{\text{cl}}\to 1/\beta\): the gain is set by
the passive feedback network, not by the messy device parameters. That single
fact is why the inverting and non-inverting formulas above depend only on
resistor ratios.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{What this buys.} By the sensitivity result of \cref{ch:feedback},
\(\dd A_{\text{cl}}/A_{\text{cl}}=(\dd A/A)/(1+L)\): the loop divides the device's
imperfections by \(1+L\). For an op-amp that is the whole design philosophy---spend
the enormous surplus gain \(A\) to buy immunity from device variation, temperature,
and distortion, and let two resistors set the answer.
\end{controlsbox}

\section{Gain--Bandwidth Product}
\label{sec:gbw}
An internally compensated op-amp is deliberately built so that one pole dominates,
\[
A(s)=\frac{A_0}{1+s/\omega_a},
\]
with an enormous DC gain \(A_0\) (often \(10^5\)) and a very low open-loop corner
\(\omega_a\) (often a few hertz). That is precisely the first-order plant analyzed
in \cref{ch:feedback}, so its result transfers unchanged: closing the loop with
constant \(\beta\) divides the DC gain by \((1+A_0\beta)\) and multiplies the corner
by the same factor, leaving the product invariant,
\[
(\text{gain})\times(\text{bandwidth})=A_0\,\omega_a=\text{constant}.
\]
For amplifiers this invariant has a name and a datasheet value: the
\textbf{gain--bandwidth product}, quoted as the \emph{unity-gain frequency}
\(f_T=A_0f_a\)---the frequency at which the op-amp can no longer provide any gain at
all. Every closed-loop configuration you build with that part lands on the same
diagonal (\cref{fig:opamp-gbw}), so choosing a closed-loop gain \emph{is} choosing a
bandwidth: doubling one halves the other.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{opamp_gbw_bode}

\label{fig:opamp-gbw}
\end{figure}

\begin{workedbox}[: reading a real part's limits]
The op-amp plotted in \cref{fig:opamp-gbw} is a common one: \(A_0=10^5\)
(\SI{100}{\decibel}) with an open-loop corner at \(f_a=\SI{10}{\hertz}\), so
\[
f_T=A_0f_a=(10^5)(\SI{10}{\hertz})=\SI{1}{\mega\hertz}.
\]
Build a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of \(100\). From
\cref{sec:beta-resistors}, that needs \(1+R_f/R_g=100\)---say
\(R_f=\SI{99}{\kilo\ohm}\) and \(R_g=\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm}\), giving
\(\beta=1/100\). Then:
\[
L(0)=A_0\beta=1000,
\qquad
A_{\mathrm{cl}}(0)=\frac{10^5}{1+1000}=99.9,
\qquad
f_c=f_a(1+A_0\beta)=\SI{10.0}{\kilo\hertz}.
\]
Three things to read off. The gain is \(99.9\) rather than \(100\)---the \(0.1\%\)
error predicted by \(1/L\). The bandwidth is \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}, not
\SI{1}{\mega\hertz}: gain \(\times\) bandwidth returns \(f_T\), as it must. And the
closed-loop pole has moved from \SI{10}{\hertz} out to \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}, a factor
of \(1001\) to the left---\cref{ch:feedback}'s first-order result with real numbers.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{exambox}
This is the practical shape of gain--bandwidth questions. An op-amp with
\(f_T=\SI{1}{\mega\hertz}\) configured for a gain of \(100\) gives about
\SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} of bandwidth---fine for audio, useless at RF. A stage needing
both high gain and wide bandwidth needs either a faster part or two cascaded stages of
modest gain (whose gains multiply while each keeps its own wider bandwidth).
\end{exambox}

As \cref{ch:feedback} showed, this single-pole model can never be destabilized by
more feedback---the pole only slides left. Instability requires the extra phase lag
of additional poles, which every real amplifier has.

\section{Stability, Phase Margin, and Gain Margin}
Real amplifiers have more than one pole. With two or more poles the loop gain
\(L(\jj\omega)\) accumulates phase lag, and if the phase reaches \(-180^\circ\)
while \(|L|\ge 1\), the subtracting junction effectively adds---the feedback has
become positive at that frequency and the closed-loop poles move toward, or
into, the right half-plane. \Cref{sec:threepoles} counts how many poles that takes:
one or two can never satisfy both conditions at once, three can, and the same
phase accounting fixes the critical gain at \(K=8\) for three identical lags.

The gain and phase margins of \cref{ch:feedback} are exactly how amplifier
stability is specified and measured: positive margins keep the closed-loop poles in
the left half-plane, small margins put them near the \(\jj\omega\) axis (ringing,
overshoot, a peaked response), and zero margin puts them on it. An op-amp datasheet's
phase-margin figure is that number for the part's own internal loop.

The same story reads on the s-plane as a root locus (\cref{fig:opamp-rootlocus}):
as loop gain rises, the closed-loop poles of a multi-pole amplifier migrate, and with
three poles two of them curve toward and cross the \(\jj\omega\) axis---the moment
small margins become ringing and then sustained oscillation.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{One amplifier, three figures.} \Cref{fig:opamp-rootlocus} is deliberately the
\emph{same} three-pole loop \(L(s)=K/(1+s/\omega_0)^3\) whose Bode margins appear in
\cref{fig:fb-margins} and whose oscillation condition appears in
\cref{fig:oscillator-loopgain}. Follow one amplifier across the three:
\begin{itemize}
\item At \(K=3\), \cref{fig:fb-margins} shows healthy gain and phase margins, and the
locus has its poles well inside the left half-plane.
\item Raising \(K\) walks the poles right along the locus, shrinking both margins.
\item At \(K=8\) the poles reach the \(\jj\omega\) axis at
\(\omega=\sqrt3\,\omega_0\); \cref{fig:oscillator-loopgain} shows the margins hitting
exactly zero, which is \(L=-1\).
\item Beyond \(K=8\) the poles are in the right half-plane and the amplifier
oscillates whether you wanted it to or not.
\end{itemize}
Margins and root locus are not two topics---they are two readings of this one
parameter sweep, which is the claim \cref{ch:feedback} opened with.
\end{controlsbox}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{opamp_rootlocus}
\caption{Root locus of the three-pole amplifier \(L(s)=K/(1+s/\omega_0)^3\) as loop
gain \(K\) increases---the same loop whose Bode margins appear in
\cref{fig:fb-margins} and whose oscillation condition appears in
\cref{fig:oscillator-loopgain}. Two closed-loop poles leave the real axis and reach
the \(\jj\omega\) axis at \(K=8\), \(\omega=\sqrt3\,\omega_0\); beyond that the
amplifier oscillates. A single-pole amplifier (one branch on the real axis) can never
do this---instability needs the extra phase lag of additional poles.}
\label{fig:opamp-rootlocus}
\end{figure}

Compensation (deliberately lowering the first pole, as in an internally
compensated op-amp) restores phase margin by making the loop gain fall through
\SI{0}{\decibel} before the second pole adds its lag. It buys stability with
bandwidth---the same trade, again.

\subsection{Neutralization: Cancelling a Loop You Did Not Ask For}
\label{sec:neutralization}
Compensation assumes you know where the loop is. RF amplifiers have a harder
problem: a feedback path exists whether or not the designer put one there.

Every active device has capacitance between its output and its input---collector to
base, drain to gate, plate to grid. That capacitance is an impedance, so it feeds a
fraction of the output back, and at RF the fraction is not small: the feedback
admittance \(\jj\omega C\) grows with frequency, so a device that is unconditionally
stable at audio can have substantial loop gain at \SI{30}{\mega\hertz}. Worse, the
feedback arrives through a \emph{capacitor}, which contributes its own
\(+90^\circ\)---so the phase condition of \cref{sec:threepoles} can be met by the
device's own strays and the stage oscillates at a frequency nobody designed.

\textbf{Neutralization} is the cure, and it is the only place in this book where
feedback is deliberately cancelled rather than shaped. A second path is added from
output to input, matched in magnitude to the stray but opposite in phase---typically
by taking the feedback from the other end of a center-tapped tank, which supplies a
signal \(180^\circ\) out of phase, through an adjustable capacitor set to match the
offending one. The two paths then sum to zero at the input:
\[
\underbrace{\jj\omega C_{\text{stray}}}_{\text{unwanted path}}
+\underbrace{\bigl(-\jj\omega C_{\text{neut}}\bigr)}_{\text{added path}}=0
\qquad\text{when}\qquad
C_{\text{neut}}=C_{\text{stray}} .
\]
With the loop gain driven to zero there is no \(L\) to satisfy \(L=-1\), and the
stage cannot oscillate at any drive level. Screen-grid tubes attack the same problem
structurally rather than by cancellation---the screen is an earthed electrode
interposed between grid and plate, which reduces \(C_{\text{stray}}\) itself---and
modern practice often avoids the issue by choosing devices whose feedback capacitance
is small enough to leave the loop gain below unity throughout.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{Three ways to keep a loop stable, and they are not interchangeable.}
Compensation reshapes \(L(\jj\omega)\) so its magnitude falls below unity before its
phase reaches \(-180^\circ\); it keeps the loop and makes it well behaved.
Neutralization \emph{removes} the loop by summing it against an equal and opposite
copy. Choosing a better device shrinks the loop's gain at source. All three are
statements about the same \(L\), which is why one framework covers an op-amp, a
regulator, and a neutralized RF power amplifier.

There is a fourth, cruder method worth naming because it is a filter in disguise: a
\textbf{parasitic suppressor}, a low-value resistor with a few turns of wire in
parallel with it, fitted in the anode or grid lead. Its impedance is
\[
Z=R\parallel s L=\frac{sLR}{R+sL},
\]
a first-order high-pass in \emph{impedance}, with its corner at \(\omega=R/L\). Below
the corner \(|sL|\ll R\) and the inductor shorts the resistor out, so
\(Z\approx sL\)---a couple of ohms, invisible at the operating frequency. Above it
\(|sL|\gg R\) and \(Z\to R\), inserting real loss. With \(R=\SI{100}{\ohm}\) and
\(L=\SI{0.1}{\micro\henry}\) the corner is at \SI{159}{\mega\hertz}, so the network
presents \(\jj\SI{8.8}{\ohm}\) at \SI{14}{\mega\hertz} but a mostly-resistive
\SI{69}{\ohm} at \SI{150}{\mega\hertz}. The suppressor does not cancel the loop; it
\emph{lowers \(|L|\) below unity in the VHF range where the parasitic wants to
oscillate}, while leaving the HF band the amplifier is for untouched. Frequency-selective
loss, placed where the trouble is.
\end{controlsbox}

\section{Oscillators: Poles Placed on the Axis}
An oscillator is a feedback loop designed to sit exactly on the stability
boundary. The closed-loop poles are the roots of \(1+L(s)=0\), so they reach the
\(\jj\omega\) axis when
\[
L(\jj\omega_{\text{osc}})=-1,
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
|L(\jj\omega_{\text{osc}})|=1,\quad
\angle L(\jj\omega_{\text{osc}})=-180^\circ,
\]
the Nyquist \(-1\) point of \cref{sec:nyquist}---the oscillator is designed so its
loop-gain curve passes exactly \emph{through} that point instead of clearing it.
This is the \emph{Barkhausen criterion}. Its familiar
``unit magnitude, zero net phase'' wording refers to the round-trip transmission
\(T=-L\), which already includes the \(-1\) of the subtracting junction; setting
\(T=1\) is the same statement as \(L=-1\). In s-plane language the closed-loop
poles are placed on the \(\jj\omega\) axis at \(\pm\jj\omega_{\text{osc}}\)---the
marginally stable case from the s-plane chapter. At start-up the design makes \(|L|>1\) so the pole pair sits slightly in
the right half-plane and the amplitude grows; a nonlinearity (device saturation,
a limiter, a lamp, or automatic gain control, AGC) then reduces the effective gain until \(|L|=1\) and the
poles settle onto the axis at a stable amplitude.

\Cref{fig:oscillator-loopgain} shows the condition on a loop-gain Bode plot: the
design places the frequency where the phase reaches \(-180^\circ\) exactly at the
\(\SI{0}{\decibel}\) (\(|L|=1\)) crossing, so \(L=-1\) is met at one frequency.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{oscillator_loopgain}
\caption{Barkhausen condition for a three-pole loop
\(L(s)=K/(1+s/\omega_0)^3\) with \(K=8\). The magnitude passes through
\SI{0}{\decibel} at the same frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{osc}}=\sqrt3\,\omega_0\)
where the phase reaches \(-180^\circ\): loop gain unity with \(180^\circ\) of lag
is \(L=-1\), so the closed-loop poles sit on the \(\jj\omega\) axis.}
\label{fig:oscillator-loopgain}
\end{figure}

The frequency-setting network sets where those poles land:
\begin{description}
\item[LC oscillators] (Colpitts, Hartley) use an LC tank; \(\omega_{\text{osc}}
\approx 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Higher tank \(Q\) means poles closer to the ideal axis
location and lower phase noise.
\item[Crystal oscillators] replace the tank with a quartz resonator of very high
\(Q\), giving excellent frequency stability.
\item[RC phase-shift / Wien-bridge] use RC networks to reach the required loop
phase; common at audio frequencies.
\end{description}
Each of these fixes \(\omega_{\text{osc}}\) by construction, which is fine for a
single frequency and useless for a transceiver that must cover a band in small steps.
\Cref{sec:pll} takes the next step: wrapping a \emph{second} loop around the
oscillator so that where its poles sit becomes a controlled variable rather than a
property of the parts.

\begin{physicalbox}
Phase noise is the frequency-domain fingerprint of how firmly the poles are
pinned to the axis. A high-\(Q\) resonator holds the pole pair tightly at
\(\pm\jj\omega_{\text{osc}}\), so the spectrum is a sharp line; a low-\(Q\) loop
lets the poles wander, smearing the line. This is why crystal and high-\(Q\) LC
oscillators are quieter.

\Cref{sec:phasenoise} puts a number on it: the skirt inherits the tank's
single-pole roll-off, so phase noise falls at \(-\SI{20}{\decibel}\) per decade of
offset and scales as \(1/Q_L^2\)---\emph{doubling the loaded \(Q\) buys
\SI{6}{\decibel}}. It also shows what that costs a receiver, since a noisy local
oscillator raises the apparent noise floor near strong signals.
\end{physicalbox}

\subsection{The Crystal Is Two Resonances}
\label{sec:crystal}
That last remark makes the quartz crystal worth a page, because everything special
about it is one equivalent circuit built from parts this book already owns---and it
has \emph{two} resonances a few parts per million apart, which is the fact all the
practical consequences hang on.

Mechanically, a quartz plate is a vibrating mass with stiffness and friction, and a
piezoelectric plate converts that motion to charge. Driven electrically it therefore
behaves as a series \(R\)--\(L_m\)--\(C_m\) branch---the \emph{motional} arm, whose
elements stand for mechanical mass, stiffness and loss. But the electrodes are also
just two plates with quartz between them, so a genuine capacitance \(C_0\) sits across
the whole thing whether the plate is vibrating or not. That is the entire model: a
series RLC shunted by \(C_0\).

Now read off the two frequencies, using nothing but \cref{ch:rlc,ch:rlcpar}. The
motional arm alone is a series resonator, so at
\[
f_s=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_mC_m}}
\]
its reactance vanishes and the crystal's impedance is a \emph{minimum}, essentially
\(R\). Slightly higher up, the motional arm has become net inductive, and an inductor
in parallel with \(C_0\) is a parallel resonator (\cref{ch:rlcpar}), so the impedance
goes through a \emph{maximum}. That happens where the motional arm's inductance
resonates with \(C_m\) and \(C_0\) in \emph{series}---because the two capacitances are
in series around that loop:
\[
f_p=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_m\dfrac{C_mC_0}{C_m+C_0}}}
=f_s\sqrt{1+\frac{C_m}{C_0}}
\;\approx\;f_s\left(1+\frac{C_m}{2C_0}\right).
\]
A crystal is thus a series resonance and a parallel resonance separated by a fraction
\(C_m/2C_0\)---and since the motional capacitance is femtofarads while the holder
capacitance is picofarads, that fraction is small. The gap is the crystal's whole
working range, and \cref{fig:crystal-impedance} is a picture of it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{crystal_impedance}
\caption{The two resonances of one quartz crystal, and the narrow window between them
that is its entire working range. \emph{Top:} the impedance magnitude falls to
\(R=\SI{15}{\ohm}\) at the series resonance and rises into the megohms at the parallel
one, \SI{15}{\kilo\hertz} (\SI{1499}{ppm}) higher. \emph{Bottom:} the reactance, which
is inductive \emph{only} inside the shaded band---which is why a Pierce or Colpitts
oscillator must run there, using the crystal as its tank inductor, and why the
specified load capacitance \(C_L\) is what chooses the exact frequency inside it.
Trimming \(C_L\) can move the oscillator only within this window, which is why a
crystal pulls by tens of parts per million and not by kilohertz. Computed from the
worked example's \(L_m=\SI{16.9}{\milli\henry}\), \(C_m=\SI{15}{\femto\farad}\),
\(C_0=\SI{5}{\pico\farad}\), \(R=\SI{15}{\ohm}\).}
\label{fig:crystal-impedance}
\end{figure}

\begin{workedbox}[: how far apart, and how high a \(Q\)]
Take \(C_m=\SI{15}{\femto\farad}\), \(C_0=\SI{5}{\pico\farad}\),
\(R=\SI{15}{\ohm}\), and pick \(L_m\) to put \(f_s\) at exactly \SI{10}{\mega\hertz};
that requires \(L_m=\SI{16.9}{\milli\henry}\). Then
\[
\frac{f_p}{f_s}=\sqrt{1+\frac{15\times10^{-15}}{5\times10^{-12}}}=1.001499,
\qquad
f_p=\SI{10.014989}{\mega\hertz},
\]
so the two resonances sit \SI{1499}{ppm}---about \SI{15}{\kilo\hertz}---apart. The
motional \(Q\) is
\[
Q=\frac{\omega_sL_m}{R}=\frac{2\pi(10^7)(0.0169)}{15}\approx 7\times10^4 ,
\]
against roughly \(200\) for a good LC tank. And notice what \(L_m\) is:
\SI{16.9}{\milli\henry} at \SI{10}{\mega\hertz}. No wound coil of that inductance has
anything remotely like that \(Q\)---which is exactly why quartz is used. The
``inductor'' is a lump of vibrating rock, and mechanical losses are far lower than
copper ones.
\end{workedbox}

Three consequences follow, and each is a pool question:

\textbf{The oscillator must be told which resonance to use.} Between \(f_s\) and
\(f_p\) the crystal looks \emph{inductive}, and that narrow band is where a
Pierce or Colpitts oscillator operates---it is using the crystal as the
high-\(Q\) inductor of its tank. Where in the band it lands depends on what
capacitance the circuit puts in series with the crystal, because that capacitance adds
to \(C_0\) in the formula above and slides \(f_p\) down toward \(f_s\). Hence a
crystal is specified with a \textbf{load capacitance} \(C_L\): the manufacturer's
marked frequency is only delivered if the circuit presents that value. With the
numbers above, \(C_L=\SI{32}{\pico\farad}\) puts the oscillator \SI{203}{ppm} above
\(f_s\) rather than the \SI{1499}{ppm} of the unloaded parallel resonance.

\textbf{The pulling range is the gap.} Trimming \(C_L\) can only move the frequency
within the \(f_s\)-to-\(f_p\) window, which is why a crystal oscillator is adjustable
by tens of parts per million and not by kilohertz. A VXO's tuning range is not a
design choice; it is \(C_m/2C_0\).

\textbf{Lattice-filter bandwidth is the gap too.} A crystal filter is built by
arranging several crystals so their series and parallel resonances interleave, and the
passband it can span is bounded by that same separation. That is why crystal filters
come in narrow bandwidths---a few hundred hertz for CW, a few kilohertz for SSB---and
why widening one means choosing crystals with a larger \(C_m/C_0\), not adjusting a
component.

\begin{exambox}
E6D asks for the equivalent circuit outright: \emph{a series RLC in parallel with a
shunt \(C\) representing electrode and stray capacitance}. Read the distractors
carefully---they permute ``series'' and ``parallel'' and swap which capacitances
combine which way, and the model above is what settles it. E7H then asks what ensures
a crystal oscillator runs on the manufacturer's marked frequency: \emph{provide the
crystal with a specified parallel capacitance}. That is \(C_L\), and the reason it
matters is the \(C_0+C_L\) in the \(f_p\) formula.
\end{exambox}

\section{The Phase-Locked Loop}
\label{sec:pll}
An oscillator places its poles on the \(\jj\omega\) axis; the obvious next question is
whether you can \emph{control} where on the axis they sit. A \textbf{phase-locked loop}
(PLL) does exactly that, and it is the most literal control loop in radio---a servo
whose controlled variable is phase.

Three blocks (\cref{fig:pll}). A \textbf{phase detector} compares the input phase with
the oscillator's and outputs a voltage proportional to the difference, with gain
\(K_d\) volts per radian. A \textbf{loop filter} \(F(s)\) smooths that error. And a
\textbf{voltage-controlled oscillator} (VCO) converts the filtered voltage into a
frequency, with gain \(K_v\) radians per second per volt.

\subsection{Why the Loop Has a Free Integrator}
The one physical insight is in that last block. A VCO's control voltage sets its
\emph{frequency}, but the phase detector compares \emph{phase}---and phase is the
integral of frequency:
\[
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\int K_v\,v(t)\,\dd t
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s)=\frac{K_v}{s}\,V(s).
\]
\emph{A VCO is an integrator.} That is the whole reason a PLL behaves the way it does,
and it is invisible if you think of the VCO as merely ``a tunable oscillator.'' The
loop gain therefore carries a free \(1/s\):
\[
\boxed{\;L(s)=\frac{K_dK_vF(s)}{s}\;}
\]

\subsection{What ``Locked'' Actually Means}
Now read the consequences off \cref{ch:feedback}. The phase error obeys
\(\Phi_e/\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}=1/(1+L)\), and with \(K=K_dK_vF(0)\) the DC loop gain,
\[
\frac{\Phi_e}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{s}{s+K}.
\]
Apply the final-value theorem to two kinds of input:
\begin{description}
\item[A phase step] (the input phase jumps by \(\Delta\phi\)) leaves \emph{zero}
steady-state error. The loop pulls the phase back exactly.
\item[A frequency step] (the input frequency moves by \(\Delta\omega\), so its phase
ramps) leaves a \emph{constant} phase error
\[
\phi_e(\infty)=\frac{\Delta\omega}{K}.
\]
\end{description}
That second result is the important one and it is easy to state carelessly. The
\emph{frequency} error goes to zero---the loop tracks the input frequency
exactly---but a \emph{static phase offset} remains, proportional to how far the VCO had
to be pulled and inversely proportional to the DC loop gain. So ``locked'' means
frequencies equal, not phases equal, and high loop gain is what makes the residual
offset small. (Adding a second integrator inside \(F(s)\)---a \emph{type-2} loop, which
is what real synthesizers use---drives that offset to zero as well, at the cost of the
extra pole's phase lag.)

\subsection{Loop Dynamics Are the Standard Second-Order System}
Take the simplest useful filter, a single pole \(F(s)=1/(1+s/\omega_f)\). Then
\(1+L=0\) gives
\[
s^2+\omega_f s+K\omega_f=0,
\]
which is \cref{ch:linsys}'s standard form with
\[
\omega_n=\sqrt{K\omega_f},
\qquad
\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_f}{K}} .
\]
Everything about the loop's transient behavior now follows from Part~II with no extra
work: how fast it acquires lock is \(\omega_n\), whether it overshoots and rings on the
way is \(\zeta\), and---note the sign---\emph{raising the loop gain lowers the
damping}, exactly as \cref{ch:feedback} found for any second-order plant under
proportional feedback. A synthesizer that rings after a band change is an underdamped
servo, nothing more exotic.

\subsection{Two Jobs the Loop Does for Free}
Because the loop equations are so simple, the PLL's two classic applications are
immediate consequences rather than separate designs.

\emph{Frequency synthesis.} Insert a divider \(\div N\) in the feedback path. Locked
now means the \emph{divided} output tracks the reference, so
\[
f_{\mathrm{out}}=N\,f_{\mathrm{ref}} .
\]
Let \(f_{\mathrm{ref}}\) come from a crystal and the output inherits crystal accuracy
while being tunable by changing an integer. That is a synthesizer, and it is why a
modern transceiver has one crystal and thousands of usable frequencies.

\emph{FM demodulation.} Suppose the input frequency wanders---which is exactly what a
frequency-modulated signal does. For the loop to stay locked, the VCO must follow, so
the control voltage must be whatever produces that frequency:
\[
v(t)=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\omega_0}{K_v} .
\]
\emph{The control voltage is proportional to the input's instantaneous frequency
deviation}---which is the demodulated audio. No detector, no discriminator: the
demodulated signal is a byproduct of the loop doing its job, and it appears at the
loop-filter output.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{The loop's bandwidth is a filter on noise, and it points two ways.} Inside the
loop bandwidth \(|L|\gg1\), so the output faithfully follows the reference: the
reference's phase noise is \emph{passed} (and multiplied by \(N\), since multiplying
frequency by \(N\) multiplies phase by \(N\)---worth \(20\log_{10}N\) decibels), while
the VCO's own noise is suppressed by \(1/(1+L)\) exactly as \cref{ch:feedback}
predicts. Outside the loop bandwidth \(|L|\ll1\), the loop cannot correct anything, so
the VCO's noise passes unaltered.

So a synthesizer's phase-noise spectrum has a characteristic shape---reference-dominated
close to the carrier, VCO-dominated far out, often with a bump where the two cross near
the loop bandwidth. It also explains a practical rule: a high division ratio \(N\)
degrades close-in phase noise by \(20\log_{10}N\), which is why synthesizers at high
output frequencies are noisier, and why \cref{sec:phasenoise}'s reciprocal mixing gets
worse on the higher bands.
\end{controlsbox}

\begin{workedbox}[: a 2-metre synthesizer]
A loop generates \SI{146.52}{\mega\hertz} from a \SI{15}{\kilo\hertz} reference, so
\[
N=\frac{146.52\times10^{6}}{15\times10^{3}}=9768 ,
\]
an integer, as a divider requires---which is precisely why reference frequencies are
chosen to divide the channel spacing evenly. The reference's phase noise is multiplied
by \(20\log_{10}(9768)\approx\SI{80}{\decibel}\) at the output---which is why the
reference must be far quieter than the specification you actually want, and why
fractional-\(N\) synthesizers (which allow a much higher reference frequency for the
same step size) exist. Suppose the loop is designed with \(K=\SI{2e4}{\per\second}\)
and \(\omega_f=\SI{5e3}{\radian\per\second}\). Then
\[
\omega_n=\sqrt{K\omega_f}=\SI{1e4}{\radian\per\second},
\qquad
\zeta=\tfrac12\sqrt{\omega_f/K}=\tfrac12\sqrt{0.25}=0.25,
\]
so \(Q=1/(2\zeta)=2\)---underdamped, and by \cref{fig:step2} it will overshoot and
ring for a couple of cycles at about \SI{1.5}{\kilo\hertz} before settling. Raising
\(K\) to reduce the static phase error would make that \emph{worse}, which is the
design tension in one line.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{exambox}
A phase-locked loop is a servo loop containing a phase detector, a loop filter (with
amplifier), and a voltage-controlled oscillator. Its two examined applications are
\textbf{frequency synthesis} and \textbf{FM demodulation}, and both fall out of the
loop equations above. Two ranges are worth distinguishing: the \textbf{lock range} is
how far the loop can hold once locked, set by the detector's usable span and the DC
loop gain; the \textbf{capture range} is how far off it can be and still \emph{acquire}
lock, which is narrower because acquisition requires the beat note between input and
VCO to fall inside the loop filter's passband. Lock range is a linear result; capture
range is not.
\end{exambox}

\section{Active Filters and Their Limits}
Op-amps can implement active RC filters that synthesize the second-order pole
pairs of \cref{ch:filters} without inductors, by using feedback to create complex
poles from resistors and capacitors. That claim deserves scrutiny: two RC stages in
cascade give two \emph{real} poles (\cref{ch:rc}), and no arrangement of resistors and
capacitors alone can produce a complex pair. Feedback is what does it.

The Sallen--Key low-pass (\cref{fig:sallenkey}) is the classic example. Writing KCL at
the two RC nodes with the buffer enforcing \(v_-=V_o\)---so that \(C_2\) feeds the
output back to the \emph{first} node rather than to ground---and eliminating the middle
node gives
\[
G(s)=\frac{\omega_0^2}{s^2+\dfrac{\omega_0}{Q}s+\omega_0^2},
\qquad
\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}},
\qquad
\frac{1}{Q}=\sqrt{\frac{C_2}{C_1}}
\left(\sqrt{\frac{R_1}{R_2}}+\sqrt{\frac{R_2}{R_1}}\right).
\]
This is precisely the standard second-order form of \cref{ch:linsys}---the same one the
LC circuit of \cref{ch:rlc} produced---so everything about \(\omega_0\), \(\zeta\), and
resonant peaking transfers unchanged. That is also what makes one stage enough to
build any order: by \cref{sec:factoring} a filter of order \(n\) is a product of
sections of exactly this form, and \cref{sec:poleplacement} tabulates the
\((\omega_0,Q)\) each section needs. An order-5 Butterworth, for instance, is two of
the stages below---one at \(Q=0.618\), one at \(Q=1.618\)---plus a single RC section
for the leftover real pole. What is new is the \emph{knob}: with equal
resistors the bracket becomes \(2\), leaving
\[
Q=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C_1}{C_2}} ,
\]
so \textbf{the capacitor ratio sets the damping}. That is the whole trick. In an LC
filter \(Q\) is set by resistance (\cref{sec:lc-lowpass}); here it is set by a ratio of
two capacitors, and the feedback path through \(C_2\) is what makes the pole pair
complex. No inductor appears anywhere.

\begin{workedbox}[: a Butterworth audio section]
Design a second-order Butterworth low-pass at \(f_0=\SI{3}{\kilo\hertz}\).
Butterworth means \(Q=1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\) (\cref{ch:filters}), so with equal
resistors,
\[
Q=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C_1}{C_2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
C_1=2\,C_2 .
\]
Choosing \(R_1=R_2=\SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}\) and imposing
\(\omega_0=1/(R\sqrt{C_1C_2})=2\pi(\SI{3}{\kilo\hertz})\) gives
\[
C_2\approx\SI{3.75}{\nano\farad},
\qquad
C_1\approx\SI{7.5}{\nano\farad}.
\]
Two resistors, two capacitors, one op-amp---and a maximally flat second-order response
that would otherwise need an inductor. Check the limits, though: at
\(f_0=\SI{3}{\kilo\hertz}\) an op-amp with \(f_T=\SI{1}{\mega\hertz}\) still has a loop
gain of roughly \(300\), so the ideal analysis holds comfortably. Push the same
topology to \SI{300}{\kilo\hertz} and it would not.
\end{workedbox}

The catch is that the same finite gain--bandwidth product limits them: near the
amplifier's own gain-crossover the ideal-op-amp assumption fails, so an active
filter that behaves at audio may be useless at RF, where LC or crystal filters
take over.

\section{Mixers: Where Linearity Ends}
\label{sec:mixers}
Every analytical tool in this book---transfer functions, poles, Bode plots, root
loci---rests on one assumption stated back in \cref{ch:linsys}: the system is
\emph{linear}. A mixer is the one circuit in radio built to violate it deliberately,
and saying so plainly is worth more than papering over it.

A linear system obeys superposition: it can change a sinusoid's amplitude and phase,
but it can never change its \emph{frequency}. That is precisely why \(G(\jj\omega)\)
is meaningful---one input frequency in, the same frequency out. A mixer must produce
frequencies that were not present at its input, so no transfer function can describe
it. What it does instead is \emph{multiply}:
\[
v_{\text{out}}\propto
\underbrace{\cos(\omega_{\text{RF}}t)}_{\text{signal}}\times
\underbrace{\cos(\omega_{\text{LO}}t)}_{\text{local oscillator}}
=\tfrac12\cos\bigl[(\omega_{\text{LO}}-\omega_{\text{RF}})t\bigr]
+\tfrac12\cos\bigl[(\omega_{\text{LO}}+\omega_{\text{RF}})t\bigr],
\]
using the product-to-sum identity. Two inputs, and the output contains their
\textbf{difference} and \textbf{sum} frequencies---neither of which was there before.
This is the whole basis of the superheterodyne receiver: multiply the incoming signal
by a local oscillator, keep the difference, and you have translated the signal to a
fixed intermediate frequency where the sharp filters of \cref{ch:filters} and the
high-gain amplifiers of this chapter can do their work at one frequency forever.

The same multiplication has one more use, and it is the reason \cref{ch:dsp} needs
almost no new theory: \emph{sampling} a signal is multiplying it by a train of
impulses, which is multiplication by every multiple of the sampling rate at once
(\cref{sec:samplingismult}). A mixer makes two frequency-shifted copies; a sampler
makes infinitely many.

Two consequences follow immediately from the algebra.

\emph{Image response.} The difference term depends only on
\(|\omega_{\text{LO}}-\omega_{\text{RF}}|\), so \emph{two} input frequencies---one
above the local oscillator and one below, equally spaced---produce the same
intermediate frequency. The unwanted one is the \textbf{image}, and no amount of
filtering \emph{after} the mixer can separate it from the wanted signal, because by
then they are identical. It must be rejected before mixing, which is what the front-end
filter is for.

The image's location follows from the same absolute value, and is worth having as a
number. If the wanted signal sits at \(f_{\text{LO}}-f_{\text{IF}}\), then the
frequency \(f_{\text{LO}}+f_{\text{IF}}\) mixes to the same place, so the two are
separated by
\[
f_{\text{image}}-f_{\text{signal}}=2f_{\text{IF}} .
\]
\emph{The image is always twice the intermediate frequency away.} That single fact
drives receiver architecture: a high first IF pushes the image far out where a
modest front-end filter can reject it, which is exactly why up-converting receivers
use first IFs above the highest received frequency.

\subsection{The Same Multiplier, Four Jobs}
A mixer's algebra does not care which port you call which, so the same circuit
appears under four names depending on what is connected and what is kept:
\begin{description}
\item[Mixer / converter] RF in, local oscillator in, keep the difference: translate
a signal to the IF. The receiving case above.
\item[Balanced modulator] audio in, carrier in, and keep \emph{only} the sum and
difference. The carrier itself is suppressed, leaving double sideband (DSB). The
suppression is the parity argument of \cref{sec:pushpull}: feed the audio to the two
halves in antiphase and subtract, and every term without an odd power of the audio---
the whole carrier included---is absent rather than trimmed away. Filter off one of the
two sidebands and the result is single sideband; this is how an SSB transmitter
generates its signal.
\item[Product detector] IF in, reinserted carrier in, keep the difference: the
audio. It is the balanced modulator run backwards, which is why an SSB receiver and
an SSB transmitter contain the same part twice.
\item[Frequency multiplier] one input, driven hard into nonlinearity, and keep a
\emph{harmonic} rather than a mixing product---the \(m f_1\) terms with a tuned
circuit selecting one.
\end{description}
Recognizing that these are one operation with different ports and different
post-filters is worth more than memorizing four block diagrams.

\emph{Intermodulation.} Real mixers and overdriven amplifiers do not multiply cleanly;
their nonlinearity contains higher-order terms, which generate combinations
\(m f_1\pm n f_2\) of any two signals present. The third-order products
\(2f_1-f_2\) and \(2f_2-f_1\) are the troublesome ones, because for two nearby
signals they land \emph{nearby too}---inside the passband, where no filter can remove
them. That is why receiver front ends are specified for intermodulation
performance, and why an amplifier driven into nonlinearity produces spurious signals
rather than merely a distorted one.

\Cref{sec:powerseries} makes that nonlinearity explicit---expanding the device as
\(y=a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3\) and driving it with two tones---and shows that every
strong-signal specification a receiver carries is a consequence of those three
coefficients.

Concretely, with \(f_1=\SI{14.20}{\mega\hertz}\) and \(f_2=\SI{14.21}{\mega\hertz}\),
the third-order products land at
\[
2f_1-f_2=\SI{14.19}{\mega\hertz},
\qquad
2f_2-f_1=\SI{14.22}{\mega\hertz},
\]
\SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} either side of the pair---the same spacing as the originals,
and hopelessly inside any filter wide enough to pass them. Even-order products, by
contrast, land near DC or near \(2f\), where filtering removes them easily. That
asymmetry is why odd-order intermodulation is the specification that matters.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{Why the two-tone test is the standard measurement.} The algebra above also
prescribes how to measure linearity. Feed the amplifier two equal tones that are
\emph{not} harmonically related---so that nothing they produce can be confused with
simple harmonic distortion---and look at the output spectrum. A perfectly linear
stage returns exactly two lines. Any third-order curvature announces itself as a
symmetric pair of new lines at \(2f_1-f_2\) and \(2f_2-f_1\), whose level relative
to the wanted tones \emph{is} the intermodulation figure. Two tones rather than one,
because a single tone would produce only harmonics---far away, easily filtered, and
therefore uninformative about the in-band products that actually matter.
\end{physicalbox}

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{The same nonlinearity, wanted and unwanted.} Class C amplification
(\cref{sec:classes}) and frequency multiplication rely on exactly the mechanism that
makes intermodulation a problem: a nonlinear device generating harmonics and mixing
products. The difference is only whether a tuned circuit afterwards keeps the product
you wanted. A high-\(Q\) tank (\cref{ch:rlcpar}) keeps one and discards the rest,
which is why Class C works at all---the nonlinearity makes a mess, and a parallel
resonator cleans it up.
\end{physicalbox}

\begin{exambox}
When a question involves two frequencies producing a third, stop looking for a
transfer function---the circuit is nonlinear and the answer is arithmetic on
frequencies: sums, differences, and \(mf_1\pm nf_2\). When a question involves one
frequency in and the same frequency out with altered amplitude or phase, the circuit
is linear and every tool in Part~II applies.
\end{exambox}

\section{Power Supplies}
A power supply looks like a different subject and is not: it is a rectifier, an RC
discharge, a low-pass filter, and a feedback loop, all of which are already in hand.
Taking it in that order costs nothing new.

\subsection{Rectification and Ripple}
A \textbf{rectifier} uses diodes to make current flow one way only. Two
arrangements matter, and the difference between them is a conduction angle:
\begin{description}
\item[Half-wave] one diode, which conducts on one polarity and blocks the other, so
it conducts through \(180^\circ\) of each input cycle. The output is a train of
pulses at the \emph{line} frequency \(f_{\text{line}}\).
\item[Full-wave] two diodes with a center-tapped transformer (or four in a bridge),
arranged so that whichever half-cycle the input is in, some path conducts. Current
therefore flows through the full \(360^\circ\), and---this is the useful
part---\emph{both} half-cycles are folded to the same polarity, so the output pulses
arrive at \(2f_{\text{line}}\).
\end{description}
That doubling is the reason full-wave rectification is worth the extra parts. The
ripple to be removed sits at twice the frequency, so the filter that removes it
needs a corner an octave higher---or, at a fixed corner, attenuates the ripple
\SI{6}{\decibel} harder (\cref{sec:decibels}). From \SI{60}{\hertz} mains, a
half-wave supply must filter \SI{60}{\hertz} and a full-wave supply
\SI{120}{\hertz}.

\subsection{The Filter Is a Low-Pass, and the Bleeder Is an RC Discharge}
What follows the rectifier is a low-pass filter built from exactly the elements of
\cref{ch:filters}: a shunt capacitor, sometimes with a series inductor (a
\emph{choke}) ahead of it to make it second order. Its job is stated in the language
of \cref{sec:lc-lowpass}---pass DC, reject everything at \(2f_{\text{line}}\) and
above---so the design rule is simply that the corner frequency must sit well below
the ripple frequency. A large capacitor is a low corner.

The \textbf{bleeder resistor} across the filter capacitor is
\cref{ch:rc} unchanged. With the supply off, capacitor and bleeder are an isolated
RC pair, so the stored charge decays as \(\ee^{-t/RC}\) and is gone after about
\(5\tau\). This is a safety component: a large filter capacitor at several hundred
volts holds \(E_C=\tfrac12CV^2\) of energy that will otherwise sit there
indefinitely.

\begin{workedbox}[: how long until it is safe?]
A \SI{100}{\micro\farad} filter capacitor charged to \SI{400}{\volt} stores
\[
E_C=\tfrac12CV^2=\tfrac12(100\times10^{-6})(400)^2=\SI{8}{\joule},
\]
which is emphatically dangerous. Fit a \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm} bleeder and
\[
\tau=RC=(10^5)(100\times10^{-6})=\SI{10}{\second},
\]
so the capacitor is effectively discharged after \(5\tau\approx\SI{50}{\second}\).
The bleeder wastes \(V^2/R=\SI{1.6}{\watt}\) continuously in normal operation---the
standard trade, safety against standing loss. Note the two competing pulls: a
smaller resistor discharges faster but wastes more, and the pole \(-1/RC\) is the
only thing being moved.
\end{workedbox}

When several capacitors are stacked in series to withstand a high voltage,
equal-value resistors across each one do double duty: they bleed the stack down, and
because they are a \emph{divider} (\cref{ch:resistive}) they force the total voltage
to share equally instead of according to the capacitors' leakage.

\subsection{Regulation Is a Feedback Loop}
A linear regulator compares its output against a reference and drives a pass element
to null the difference---so it is literally \cref{ch:feedback}'s block diagram with
a transistor as the forward path. Everything from that chapter therefore applies:
the large-loop-gain limit is why the output tracks the reference rather than the
transistor's parameters, the sensitivity result \(1/(1+L)\) is what ``line and load
regulation'' measure, and the margins are why a regulator with too much loop gain
or a marginal output capacitor \emph{rings}. Switching converters replace the
dissipative pass element with a duty-cycle-modulated switch and an LC filter; the
high switching frequency is chosen deliberately, because a higher ripple frequency
means the same attenuation from far smaller reactive components.

\subsection{Thermal Runaway Is a Loop Too---at DC}
\label{sec:thermalrunaway}
The most destructive feedback loop in a transmitter has no wires. A silicon
transistor's collector current rises with temperature (its \(V_{BE}\) falls by about
\SI{2}{\milli\volt} per kelvin at fixed current), the current raises the dissipation,
and the dissipation raises the temperature. That is a closed loop, so
\cref{ch:feedback}'s criterion applies to it---at zero frequency. Multiply the three
gains around it:
\[
L_{\text{th}}
=\underbrace{\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial T}}_{\text{device}}
\cdot\underbrace{\theta_{JA}}_{\text{heat sink}}
\cdot\underbrace{V_{CE}}_{\text{operating point}}
\qquad\text{[dimensionless]},
\]
where \(\theta_{JA}\) is the junction-to-ambient thermal resistance in kelvin per
watt. If \(L_{\text{th}}\ge1\) the loop is regenerative and the device destroys
itself; below \(1\) a temperature excursion decays. \emph{Thermal runaway is
\(L=+1\) at DC}, the same instability condition as an oscillator with the sign of the
feedback reversed.

Read the three factors as three independent cures, which is exactly how they are used.
An \textbf{emitter resistor} attacks the first: it is local negative feedback, and it
holds \(\partial I_C/\partial T\approx(\SI{2}{\milli\volt\per\kelvin})/R_E\), so
\SI{10}{\ohm} makes the device term \SI{0.2}{\milli\ampere\per\kelvin} instead of
tens. A \textbf{heat sink} attacks the second, dropping \(\theta_{JA}\) from perhaps
\SI{50}{\kelvin\per\watt} to \SI{5}{}. And running at \textbf{lower \(V_{CE}\)}---or
in a switching class, where \cref{sec:switching} showed \(V_{CE}\) and \(I_C\) are
never both large---attacks the third. With \(R_E=\SI{10}{\ohm}\),
\(\theta_{JA}=\SI{5}{\kelvin\per\watt}\) and \(V_{CE}=\SI{20}{\volt}\),
\(L_{\text{th}}=0.02\); remove the emitter resistor and the same stage runs at
\(L_{\text{th}}=2\) and fails.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{Positive feedback, and why the fix is a resistor.} Every other loop in this
chapter subtracts; this one adds, so instead of \(1+L\) dividing the sensitivity it is
\(1-L\), which blows up as \(L\to1\) rather than settling. The emitter resistor works
because it inserts an ordinary \emph{negative} loop inside the thermal one, reducing
the forward gain the thermal loop has available. Bias stability and thermal stability
are therefore the same design problem seen at two time scales---microseconds for the
electrical loop, seconds for the thermal one---and it is the same algebra either way.
\end{controlsbox}

\begin{exambox}
Power-supply questions usually reduce to one of three facts. Full-wave rectification
puts ripple at \emph{twice} the line frequency and half-wave at the line frequency.
The filter is a low-pass, so bigger capacitance means a lower corner and less
ripple. And the bleeder is an RC discharge whose \(\tau=RC\) sets how long the supply
stays lethal after switch-off.
\end{exambox}

\subsection{Hysteresis: Positive Feedback Used on Purpose}
\label{sec:hysteresis}
\Cref{sec:thermalrunaway} was positive feedback as a hazard. Used deliberately, the
same sign gives a comparator a property it badly needs. A bare comparator driven by a
slowly moving, slightly noisy signal chatters: near the threshold, noise carries the
input back and forth across it and the output rattles. The cure is to make the
threshold move away from the input as soon as it is crossed.

Feed a fraction \(\beta=R_1/(R_1+R_2)\) of the output back to the \emph{non-inverting}
input, with the signal on the inverting input. The output sits at one of its rails
\(\pm V_{\text{sat}}\), so the threshold is not zero but
\[
V_{\text{th}}=\beta\,V_{\text{out}}=\pm\beta V_{\text{sat}},
\]
and the hysteresis width---the gap between the rising and falling thresholds---is
\[
\Delta V=2\beta V_{\text{sat}} .
\]
With \(\pm\SI{12}{\volt}\) rails, \(R_1=\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm}\) and
\(R_2=\SI{99}{\kilo\ohm}\), \(\beta=0.01\) and the thresholds sit at
\(\pm\SI{0.12}{\volt}\): once the input pushes the output high, the input must fall
\SI{0.24}{\volt} to bring it back. Noise smaller than that cannot cause a second
transition.

Two things follow from the sign. Because the feedback is positive, the loop gain
exceeds unity during the transition, so the switching is \emph{regenerative}---the
output does not creep across, it snaps, and the transition is faster than the
comparator's own slew limit would suggest. And because \(\Delta V\) is set by a
resistor ratio and the rail voltage, the noise immunity is a design choice traded
directly against resolution: every millivolt of hysteresis is a millivolt of input
change the circuit is deliberately blind to.

\begin{exambox}
For active-circuit questions, keep three layers separate: small-signal gain,
power handling, and stability. Feedback questions almost always reduce to one
idea---\emph{loop gain}: large loop gain buys predictable gain, wide bandwidth,
and low distortion; too much phase lag at unity loop gain costs stability; and an
oscillator is the deliberate limiting case with the poles parked on the
\(\jj\omega\) axis. Positive feedback is the same criterion with the sign flipped, and
it appears twice: as a fault in \cref{sec:thermalrunaway} and as a feature in
\cref{sec:hysteresis}.
\end{exambox}
