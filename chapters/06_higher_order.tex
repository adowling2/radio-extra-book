\chapter{Higher-Order Systems: Cascades, Pole Patterns, and Delay}
\label{ch:highorder}
Everything in Part~II so far has had one or two energy stores. Real radio circuits
often have more: a five-pole filter, a three-stage amplifier, a feed line with
infinitely many modes. This chapter closes Part~II by extending the toolkit to
arbitrary order, and its thesis is deliberately anticlimactic---\emph{almost no new
machinery is required}. Every high-order linear system factors into the first- and
second-order pieces of \cref{ch:linsys}, and every high-order frequency response is
those pieces' responses added up in decibels and degrees.

That claim is worth a chapter precisely because it is so useful. It means the two
systems you already understand are not special cases to be outgrown; they are the
alphabet. It also settles three loose ends: where \(-20n\)~dB/decade comes from, why
\cref{ch:feedback} insisted that instability needs three poles, and what ``linear
phase'' means when a filter datasheet claims it.

\section{Order Is the Number of Independent Energy Stores}
The counting rule is the same one \cref{ch:linsys} used, just not stopped at two. A
system with \(n\) independent energy stores has \(n\) state variables, so its state
matrix \(A\) is \(n\times n\), its characteristic polynomial
\(\det(sI-A)\) has degree \(n\), and it therefore has exactly \(n\) poles:
\[
\dot{\state}=A\state+Bu,
\qquad
\state\in\mathbb{R}^n
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
n \text{ poles} .
\]
Nothing about \(n=5\) is harder than \(n=2\) in principle. What changes is that
factoring a fifth-degree polynomial by hand is unpleasant---which is exactly why the
next section matters.

\section{Every High-Order System Is a Cascade of the Two You Know}
\label{sec:factoring}
Here is the structural fact that makes higher order tractable.

A physical system has \emph{real} coefficients---resistances, inductances, masses are
real numbers---so its characteristic polynomial has real coefficients. And a real
polynomial's complex roots always occur in \textbf{conjugate pairs}: if
\(\sigma+\jj\omega\) is a root, so is \(\sigma-\jj\omega\). (Otherwise multiplying the
factors out would leave imaginary coefficients.) Therefore every real polynomial
factors into
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{real linear factors} \((s+1/\tau_k)\), one per real pole, and
\item \textbf{irreducible real quadratic factors}
\(\bigl(s^2+2\zeta_k\omega_{0k}s+\omega_{0k}^2\bigr)\), one per conjugate pair.
\end{itemize}
So any transfer function of order \(n\) can be written as a \emph{product}:
\[
\boxed{\;
G(s)=K\prod_k\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\tau_ks}}_{\text{first order}}
\prod_j\underbrace{\frac{\omega_{0j}^2}{s^2+2\zeta_j\omega_{0j}s+\omega_{0j}^2}}_{\text{second order}} \;}
\]
An even-order system is \(n/2\) quadratic sections; an odd-order system is
\((n-1)/2\) quadratics plus one real pole. \emph{There is no third kind of building
block, at any order.}

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{Why this is the whole chapter in one idea.} A fifth-order filter is not a new
object requiring new theory. It is two second-order systems and one first-order system
in series, each of which \cref{ch:linsys,ch:splane} already characterized completely.
Reading a high-order design therefore reduces to reading a list of
\((\omega_0,\zeta)\) pairs---and \emph{building} one reduces to building those
sections and connecting them, which is exactly how active filters are made
(\cref{ch:active}).
\end{controlsbox}

\section{Cascades Add in Decibels and in Degrees}
\label{sec:cascadeadd}
Now the frequency-domain payoff. \Cref{ch:linsys} showed that cascaded blocks
\emph{multiply}, \(G=G_1G_2\). Multiplication of complex numbers multiplies magnitudes
and adds angles, so
\[
|G(\jj\omega)|=|G_1(\jj\omega)|\cdot|G_2(\jj\omega)|,
\qquad
\angle G=\angle G_1+\angle G_2 .
\]
Take \(20\log_{10}\) of the magnitude equation and the product becomes a sum, because
that is what logarithms do:
\[
\boxed{\;
20\log_{10}|G|=20\log_{10}|G_1|+20\log_{10}|G_2|,
\qquad
\angle G=\angle G_1+\angle G_2 \;}
\]
\textbf{This is the reason Bode plots use decibels.} On a dB scale, cascading is
addition---so a high-order Bode plot is literally the sum of its sections' plots, and
the sketching rules of \cref{ch:splane} (each pole bends the slope down by
\SI{20}{\decibel}/decade, each zero up) are simply this addition performed
graphically.

\subsection{Deriving the \texorpdfstring{\(-20n\)}{-20n} dB/Decade Roll-Off}
The rule quoted throughout the book now follows in one step. Far above all its
corners, each first-order section contributes \(-\SI{20}{\decibel}\) per decade
(\cref{sec:asymptotes}), and each second-order section contributes twice that. For
\(n\) poles total, the contributions add:
\[
\underbrace{(-20)+(-20)+\cdots}_{n\text{ terms}}
=-20n\ \si{\decibel}\text{ per decade}.
\]
Likewise the phase lag accumulates \(-90^\circ\) per pole, approaching
\(-90n^\circ\) far above the corners. Both facts are consequences of
\cref{sec:cascadeadd} and nothing more.

\begin{workedbox}[: three identical lags]
Cascade three identical first-order stages, each with corner \(\omega_0\):
\[
G(s)=\frac{1}{(1+s/\omega_0)^3}.
\]
Far above \(\omega_0\), the magnitude falls at \(-\SI{60}{\decibel}\) per decade and
the phase approaches \(-270^\circ\). At the corner itself each stage is down
\SI{3.01}{\decibel} and lagging \(45^\circ\), so the cascade is down
\(3\times3.01\approx\SI{9}{\decibel}\) and lagging \(135^\circ\)---all three numbers
obtained by addition, with no new algebra. Keep this system in mind; the next section
shows it is the one that can oscillate.
\end{workedbox}

\section{Why Three Poles Can Oscillate and Two Cannot}
\label{sec:threepoles}
\Cref{ch:feedback} asserted that genuine instability needs three or more poles. With
\cref{sec:cascadeadd} in hand we can prove it, and the proof is just phase
accounting.

Recall the condition for closed-loop poles on the \(\jj\omega\) axis:
\(L(\jj\omega)=-1\), meaning \(|L|=1\) and \(\angle L=-180^\circ\) at the \emph{same}
frequency. Since each first-order lag contributes at most \(-90^\circ\):
\begin{description}
\item[One pole] the phase approaches \(-90^\circ\) but never reaches
\(-180^\circ\). The condition is unreachable at any gain---the loop is
unconditionally stable, as \cref{ch:feedback} found by moving the pole directly.
\item[Two poles] the phase approaches \(-180^\circ\) only asymptotically, as
\(\omega\to\infty\), where the magnitude has already fallen to zero. So \(|L|=1\) and
\(\angle L=-180^\circ\) never coincide: still unconditionally stable, though the poles
can be driven arbitrarily close to the axis (which is why a two-pole loop can ring
hard without ever oscillating).
\item[Three poles] the phase reaches \(-180^\circ\) at a \emph{finite} frequency, where
the magnitude is still nonzero. Now enough gain will satisfy both conditions at once,
and the loop oscillates.
\end{description}

For the three-lag cascade of the box above, put \(L(s)=K/(1+s/\omega_0)^3\). The phase
is three times a single lag's:
\[
\angle L=-3\arctan\!\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}=-180^\circ
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\arctan\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}=60^\circ
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega_{\mathrm{osc}}=\sqrt3\,\omega_0 .
\]
At that frequency each stage has magnitude
\(1/|1+\jj\sqrt3|=1/2\), so the cascade has magnitude \(K/8\). Setting \(|L|=1\):
\[
\boxed{\;K=8\quad\text{puts the poles exactly on the }\jj\omega\text{ axis at }
\omega=\sqrt3\,\omega_0 . \;}
\]
Below \(K=8\) the loop is stable with positive margins; above it, the pole pair is in
the right half-plane. This single system is the one plotted three ways in
\cref{fig:fb-margins} (\(K=3\), healthy margins),
\cref{fig:opamp-rootlocus} (the poles migrating, crossing at \(K=8\)), and
\cref{fig:oscillator-loopgain} (\(K=8\), margins exactly zero)---and now the arithmetic
behind all three lives here.

\section{Pure Delay: Phase Lag Without Any Poles}
A delay of \(T\) seconds is not a pole at all, yet it destabilizes loops more
effectively than poles do. Its transfer function is
\[
G(s)=\ee^{-sT},
\qquad
|G(\jj\omega)|=1,
\qquad
\angle G(\jj\omega)=-\omega T .
\]
Read those two facts together: the magnitude is \emph{never} attenuated, while the
phase lag grows \emph{without bound} and linearly in \(\omega\). So a delay crosses
\(-180^\circ\), then \(-540^\circ\), then \(-900^\circ\), forever, always at full
amplitude. Any loop containing a real delay can be made to oscillate, and at
infinitely many frequencies. This is why transport lag is the classic difficulty in
process control, and why it deserves mention here: a transmission line
(\cref{ch:lines}) \emph{is} a delay, which is the physical reason it has infinitely
many resonances rather than a handful.

\section{Where Designers Put the Poles}
\label{sec:poleplacement}
Given that a high-order response is a product of sections, designing one means
choosing where the poles go. The named filter families are exactly rules for that
choice, and the archetype is worth deriving because the answer is so clean.

The \textbf{Butterworth} family is defined by a magnitude specification---as flat as
possible in the passband:
\[
|G(\jj\omega)|^2=\frac{1}{1+(\omega/\omega_c)^{2n}} .
\]
Setting the denominator to zero to find the poles gives
\((s/\jj\omega_c)^{2n}=-1\), whose \(2n\) roots are equally spaced around a circle of
radius \(\omega_c\). Keeping the \(n\) in the left half-plane (the others would be
unstable) leaves \(n\) poles \emph{equally spaced on a semicircle of radius
\(\omega_c\)}, separated by \(\pi/n\). That is the picture
\cref{fig:butterworth-poles} shows.

Now factor that pattern into sections using \cref{sec:factoring}. A pole pair sitting
at angle \(\phi\) from the negative real axis has
\(\cos\phi=\zeta\) (\cref{ch:splane}), hence \(Q=1/(2\cos\phi)\). Applying that to the
equally spaced Butterworth angles gives the table every filter designer works from:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}L{0.10\textwidth}L{0.30\textwidth}Y@{}}
\toprule
Order & Sections & Section \(Q\) (all at \(\omega_0=\omega_c\)) \\
\midrule
2 & one quadratic & \(Q=0.707\) \\
3 & one quadratic + one real pole & \(Q=1.000\); real pole at \(-\omega_c\) \\
4 & two quadratics & \(Q=0.541\), \(Q=1.307\) \\
5 & two quadratics + one real pole & \(Q=0.618\), \(Q=1.618\); real pole at \(-\omega_c\) \\
6 & three quadratics & \(Q=0.518\), \(Q=0.707\), \(Q=1.932\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Three things are worth noticing. First, the fifth-order values \(0.618\) and \(1.618\)
are the golden ratio and its reciprocal---a consequence of the pentagonal spacing, and
a good sanity check that you have the pattern right. Second, higher order always
introduces at least one \emph{high-\(Q\)} section: order 6 needs a \(Q=1.93\) stage,
which will visibly peak on its own even though the assembled filter is maximally flat.
Third, this table is the bridge between \cref{ch:filters}, which shows what the
families do, and \cref{ch:active}, which builds one Sallen--Key stage per row---an
order-5 Butterworth is two Sallen--Key stages with \(Q=0.618\) and \(Q=1.618\) plus
one RC section.

\textbf{Chebyshev} filters keep the same idea but place the poles on an
\emph{ellipse} pushed closer to the \(\jj\omega\) axis, raising every section's \(Q\).
The result is a sharper transition at the price of passband ripple and worse transient
behavior---which is now predictable rather than surprising, since higher \(Q\) has
meant more ringing since \cref{ch:linsys}. \Cref{ch:filters} takes the comparison up
from here.

\section{Zeros at Higher Order}
Poles are not the only tool. A \textbf{zero} at \(s=\jj\omega_z\) drives the response
to zero at that frequency and bends the magnitude slope \emph{up} by
\SI{20}{\decibel}/decade---the mirror of a pole, and additive in dB by
\cref{sec:cascadeadd}. A system with \(n\) poles and \(m\) zeros rolls off at
\(-20(n-m)\)~dB/decade far out, but can have deep, narrow nulls placed exactly where
interference lives.

This is a genuine design trade: zeros buy stopband \emph{depth} at specific
frequencies, where poles buy stopband \emph{slope} everywhere. Elliptic filters spend
both. \Cref{ch:filters} shows what this buys in practice---and
\cref{ch:measurement} shows a zero being placed on top of a pole to cancel it
entirely.

\section{Phase Delay, Group Delay, and Waveform Fidelity}
\label{sec:groupdelay}
One axis of the filter trade-off has not yet been quantified: phase. A filter that
passes every frequency at the right amplitude can still ruin a waveform, if it delays
different frequencies by different amounts.

For a sinusoid at \(\omega\), a phase shift \(\phi(\omega)=\angle G(\jj\omega)\) is
equivalent to a time shift of \(-\phi/\omega\). But a real signal is many
frequencies, and what matters is how the \emph{envelope} moves. That is the
\textbf{group delay}:
\[
\boxed{\;\tau_g(\omega)=-\frac{\dd\phi}{\dd\omega}\;}
\]
If \(\tau_g\) is \emph{constant}, every frequency component is delayed equally: the
waveform arrives later but otherwise unchanged. Constant group delay means
\(\phi=-\omega\tau_g\), i.e.\ phase falling \emph{linearly} with frequency---which is
what ``\textbf{linear phase}'' means, and why the two terms are used
interchangeably. Note that a pure delay (previous section) has exactly this property,
with \(\tau_g=T\): a delay is the ideal distortionless element.

Group delays add across a cascade, since phases do. So the design question becomes
whether a family's poles can be placed to flatten \(\tau_g\)---and the
\textbf{Bessel} family is defined by doing exactly that, accepting a gentle amplitude
roll-off in exchange. Chebyshev sits at the opposite corner: its high-\(Q\) sections
make \(\tau_g\) peak sharply near cutoff, so a pulse through a Chebyshev filter rings
and spreads.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{Which one you want depends on what you are sending.} For a narrow SSB or CW
signal, amplitude selectivity is everything and phase distortion is inaudible---use
Chebyshev or elliptic. For anything where pulse shape carries the information (digital
modes, video, or a transmitted waveform whose envelope must stay clean), group-delay
flatness matters and Bessel or a linear-phase FIR filter is the right choice. This is
the third axis of \cref{fig:filter-families}, and now it has a number attached.
\end{physicalbox}

\section{The Limit of Infinitely Many Poles}
Push the order up without bound and something qualitatively new appears. A cascade of
\(n\) identical lags, each of time constant \(T/n\), tends to a pure delay of \(T\) as
\(n\to\infty\)---infinitely many poles conspiring to produce exactly the
\(\ee^{-sT}\) of the delay section. That is not an idle limit: it is what a
transmission line \emph{is}. \Cref{ch:lines} models a line as an infinite ladder of
infinitesimal \(L\) and \(C\) sections, finds a wave equation rather than an ODE, and
sees the infinite family of resonances directly in the periodic poles of
\(\tan\beta\ell\). The lumped machinery of Part~II reaches its edge there, and the
transition is continuous.

\begin{exambox}
The exam does not ask for fifth-order pole coordinates. It asks for the roll-off of an
\(n\)-pole filter (\(-20n\)~dB/decade, \cref{sec:cascadeadd}), which family is sharp
versus flat versus linear-phase (\cref{sec:poleplacement,sec:groupdelay}), what a
notch or trap does (zeros), and why an amplifier with too much loop gain oscillates
(\cref{sec:threepoles}). All four are consequences of one idea: a high-order system is
its first- and second-order sections, added up in decibels and degrees.
\end{exambox}
