\chapter{RL Circuits}
\label{ch:rl}
An RL circuit is the current-domain sibling of the RC circuit: it is the same
\emph{first-order system} of \cref{ch:linsys}, but now the natural state is the
inductor current \(i_L\), which holds the stored magnetic energy.

\section{Where RL Circuits Appear}
Inductors most often appear \emph{with} capacitors, in the resonant circuits of
\cref{ch:rlc,ch:rlcpar}---so it is fair to ask where an inductor works against a
resistance alone. Several places, and they matter:
\begin{description}
\item[RF chokes] a coil in a bias or supply lead, there to pass DC while blocking
RF. What matters is the choke's inductance against the resistance of the path it
feeds, and \(\tau=L/R\) sets how well it isolates.
\item[Relay and solenoid coils] a coil switched by a transistor is an inductance in
series with its own winding resistance, nothing more. Its \(L/R\) governs how fast
it pulls in---and its stored energy is why the switch needs a flyback diode.
\item[Transformer and motor windings] modeled as leakage inductance in series with
winding resistance, which is what limits how fast the current can change.
\item[Loading coils and conductor loss] the coil in a shortened antenna is an
inductance whose unavoidable series resistance sets its \(Q\), directly limiting
efficiency and bandwidth (\cref{ch:lines}).
\item[Every real inductor] a coil \emph{is} an RL circuit by itself, since its
winding resistance sits in series with its inductance; its self-capacitance only
matters near self-resonance.
\end{description}
The RL circuit therefore earns its own chapter both because these cases are real and
because the RC/RL duality it exposes is the fastest way to remember either one.

\section{The Circuit}
A source \(u(t)\) drives a resistor \(R\) in series with an inductor \(L\)
(\cref{fig:rl-schematic}). The state is the inductor current \(i\), but the
\emph{output} is a \textbf{voltage}---the one across the resistor:
\[
v_o=v_R=R\,i .
\]
Since \(v_R\) is simply \(R\) times the state, this output is a scaled picture of
the inductor current, which is why it is called low-pass ``in the current sense.''
Taking \(v_o\) across the inductor instead gives a high-pass response.

\section{From Schematic to Differential Equation}
KVL gives the source as the sum of the inductor and resistor voltages, and the
inductor law relates its voltage to its rate of current change:
\[
u(t)=L\frac{\dd i}{\dd t}+R\,i(t)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\,\frac{L}{R}\frac{\dd i}{\dd t}+i=\frac{1}{R}\,u(t).\,}
\]

\section{State-Space Form and the Generic Match}
With the single state \(x=i\), and remembering that the output is the resistor
voltage,
\[
\dot{x}=-\frac{R}{L}\,x+\frac{1}{L}\,u,
\qquad
y=v_o=v_R=R\,x .
\]
The output equation is what distinguishes this from a bare current model: \(y\) is
the resistor voltage, so \(C_{\text{out}}=R\) and \(D=0\). Transforming the state
equation gives the transfer function to the current, and multiplying by \(R\) gives
the one to the output:
\[
\frac{I(s)}{U(s)}=\frac{1/L}{s+R/L},
\qquad
G(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{U(s)}=R\cdot\frac{1/L}{s+R/L}=\frac{1}{1+(L/R)s},
\]
a unity-DC-gain first-order low-pass---the same form as the RC circuit's
\(G_C\). Comparing the state equation with \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) from
\cref{ch:linsys} (taking \(y\) as the \emph{current}, whose DC gain is \(1/R\)),
\[
\tau=\frac{L}{R},
\qquad
K=\frac{1}{R},
\qquad
s_{\text{pole}}=-\frac{R}{L}=-\frac{1}{\tau}.
\]
It is the same first-order system as the RC circuit, with the time constant
\(L/R\) in place of \(RC\). Note the duality: RC stores energy in a voltage and
its time constant grows with \(R\); RL stores energy in a current and its time
constant \emph{shrinks} with \(R\).

\section{Transient Response}
For a DC step of height \(E\), and for decay from an initial current \(I_0\),
\[
i(t)=\frac{E}{R}\left(1-\ee^{-Rt/L}\right),
\qquad
i(t)=I_0\,\ee^{-Rt/L}.
\]
This is again the first-order response of \cref{ch:linsys}, read as an inductor
current (\cref{fig:rl-transient}): after one time constant \(\tau=L/R\) the
current has reached \(63.2\%\) of its final value. Inductor current cannot jump
under finite voltage---the dual of the capacitor's continuous voltage.

\section{Frequency Response and the Root Locus}
The inductor impedance is \(Z_L=\jj\omega L\), which grows with frequency, so an
inductor opposes rapid current changes and looks like a short at DC. Output across
\(R\) is low-pass (\cref{fig:bode1}); across \(L\) it is high-pass, identical in
shape to the RC high-pass of \cref{fig:rc-highpass}, with corner
\(\omega_c=R/L\). The single pole \(-R/L\) rides the negative real axis with the
same geometry as the RC pole (\cref{fig:rc-rootlocus}); as \(R\)
increases the pole moves \emph{left} (faster), the mirror of the RC case.

\begin{workedbox}[: sizing a choke]
A bias line feeding a \SI{50}{\ohm} path must pass DC while presenting at least
\SI{500}{\ohm} of reactance at \SI{14}{\mega\hertz}. From \(X_L=\omega L\),
\[
L\ge\frac{X_L}{\omega}=\frac{500}{2\pi(14\times10^6)}\approx\SI{5.7}{\micro\henry},
\]
so a standard \SI{10}{\micro\henry} choke (\(X_L\approx\SI{880}{\ohm}\)) is
comfortable. Read the same part as a first-order system and the choice is the
corner frequency: \(\tau=L/R=\SI{0.2}{\micro\second}\) puts \(f_c\approx
\SI{796}{\kilo\hertz}\), and \SI{14}{\mega\hertz} is \(1.25\) decades past it,
hence about \SI{25}{\decibel} of attenuation. Two vocabularies, one pole.
\emph{But check \cref{sec:selfresonance} before trusting it:} a
\SI{10}{\micro\henry} coil with \SI{2}{\pico\farad} of winding capacitance
self-resonates near \SI{36}{\mega\hertz}, above which it stops being an inductor
at all.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{Connection.} RC and RL are the two first-order lags of circuit theory.
Same pole structure, same Bode shape, same step response---only the state
variable (voltage vs current) and the direction of the \(R\)-dependence differ.
Learn one and you have both.
\end{controlsbox}

\section{Energy Interpretation}
The inductor stores \(E_L=\tfrac12 Li^2\) in its magnetic field. When current is
interrupted, that energy must go somewhere.

\begin{energybox}
Because \(E_L=\tfrac12Li^2\) cannot vanish instantly, breaking an inductive current
forces the inductor to produce whatever voltage is needed to keep the current
flowing---which is why an opening switch arcs, and why flyback diodes and snubbers
exist. The dual statement for a capacitor is that shorting it produces whatever
\emph{current} is needed to keep its voltage continuous.
\end{energybox}

\begin{exambox}
Remember the natural state: capacitor voltage and inductor current. That one
sentence tells you which quantity is continuous and which differential equation
to write.
\end{exambox}

\section{Worked Example}
A \(\SI{10}{\micro\henry}\) inductor is in series with a \(\SI{50}{\ohm}\)
resistor. The time constant and pole are
\[
\tau=\frac{L}{R}=\frac{10\times10^{-6}}{50}=\SI{2.0e-7}{\second},
\qquad
s_{\text{pole}}=-\frac{R}{L}=-\SI{5e6}{\per\second},
\]
so the corner frequency is \(f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\approx\SI{796}{\kilo\hertz}\). At
\SI{14}{\mega\hertz}---well above the corner---the reactance
\(X_L=2\pi(14\times10^6)(10\times10^{-6})\approx\SI{880}{\ohm}\) dominates the
\SI{50}{\ohm} resistor, so the inductor's impedance sets the behavior.

\Cref{fig:rl-worked} plots this specific circuit. On the magnitude curve the
\SI{14}{\mega\hertz} point sits about \SI{25}{\decibel} down---consistent with being
roughly \(1.25\) decades past the corner at \(-\SI{20}{\decibel}\) per decade---which
is exactly the choke behavior this combination is used for: it passes DC and audio
while attenuating HF.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{How does adjusting \(R\) change the dynamics?} As with the RC circuit, the
single real pole can only slide along the real axis---no value of \(R\) produces
ringing. But the \emph{direction} is reversed: here \(\tau=L/R\), so increasing
\(R\) \emph{shortens} the time constant and moves the pole left (faster, higher
cutoff), whereas in the RC circuit increasing \(R\) lengthened \(\tau\) and moved
the pole right. This is the RC/RL duality in one sentence, and it is the detail most
often misremembered: for a choke, \emph{lowering} the circuit resistance is what
makes the isolation better at low frequencies.
\end{physicalbox}
