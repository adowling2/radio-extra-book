\chapter{Devices, Bias, and Regulation}
\label{ch:devices}
Passive circuits shape a signal; active devices supply the controlled current that
amplifies, switches, oscillates, and regulates it.  The useful mathematical model
is local: choose a DC operating point, linearize the device there, and use the
resulting controlled source with the feedback ideas of \cref{ch:feedback}.

\section{Bias Turns a Nonlinear Device into a Small-Signal Model}
\label{sec:devicebias}
A diode's current rises approximately exponentially with voltage,
\[
I_D=I_S\left(\ee^{V_D/(nV_T)}-1\right),
\]
where \(V_T\) is the thermal voltage (about \SI{26}{\milli\volt} near room
temperature) and \(n\) accounts for device physics.  The exponential is not a
linear resistor.  But at a selected bias current \(I_D\), a small voltage change
has incremental slope \(\dd I_D/\dd V_D\), giving the small-signal resistance
\[
\boxed{\quad r_d\approx\frac{nV_T}{I_D}.\quad}
\]
The word ``small'' matters: this model describes perturbations around the chosen
DC point, not a large rectified waveform crossing from cutoff to conduction.

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) uses a forward-biased base--emitter junction
to control collector current.  Near its operating point,
\[
I_C\approx I_S\ee^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad
\boxed{\quad g_m=\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{BE}}
\approx\frac{I_C}{V_T}.\quad}
\]
Thus a small \(v_{be}\) produces collector current \(i_c=g_mv_{be}\); a collector
resistor converts that controlled current back into voltage.  The base also draws
current, approximately \(I_B=I_C/\beta\).  A field-effect transistor instead uses
an electric field at an insulated or reverse-biased gate to control drain current.
Its gate current is usually much smaller, and its local small-signal description
has the same useful form: \(i_d=g_mv_{gs}\).  A vacuum tube is another controlled
current source: grid voltage controls plate current.  The technologies differ; the
load-line and feedback reasoning is the same.

\section{Load Lines and Emitter or Source Degeneration}
\label{sec:loadlines}
Consider a common-emitter BJT stage with collector resistor \(R_C\) and supply
\(V_{CC}\).  KVL constrains the possible DC points to
\[
V_{CE}=V_{CC}-I_CR_C.
\]
This straight \textbf{load line} intersects the transistor's nonlinear current--
voltage curves at the quiescent, or \(Q\), point.  Bias near the middle of the
available voltage swing permits a sinusoid to move both upward and downward before
cutoff or saturation clips it.  Moving the point toward cutoff is how Class B or C
operation trades linearity for efficiency (\cref{sec:conductionangle}).

An emitter resistor \(R_E\) adds local negative feedback.  If collector current
rises, emitter voltage rises, reducing \(V_{BE}\) and opposing the rise.  In the
small-signal model the emitter's intrinsic resistance is
\(r_e\approx1/g_m=V_T/I_C\), and the approximate voltage gain becomes
\[
\boxed{\quad A_v\approx-\frac{R_C}{r_e+R_E}.\quad}
\]
The minus sign is the common-emitter inversion.  The resistor lowers gain but makes
that gain, the input resistance, and the DC current less dependent on the poorly
controlled transistor parameters.  This is the same gain-versus-robustness bargain
as \(A/(1+A\beta)\), executed with one resistor rather than a separate feedback
network.  A source resistor performs the corresponding job in a FET stage.

\begin{workedbox}[: how a resistor stabilizes a BJT]
At \(I_C=\SI{1}{\milli\ampere}\), \(r_e\approx
\SI{26}{\milli\volt}/\SI{1}{\milli\ampere}=\SI{26}{\ohm}\).  With
\(R_C=\SI{4.7}{\kilo\ohm}\) and no unbypassed emitter resistor, the ideal local
gain is about \(-4700/26=-181\).  Adding \(R_E=\SI{470}{\ohm}\) changes it to
about \(-4700/(26+470)=-9.5\).  The stage gives up gain, but a modest change in
transconductance no longer makes its behavior unpredictable.  A bypass capacitor
can restore AC gain over a chosen band while retaining DC bias stabilization.
\end{workedbox}

\section{Rectification and Regulation Are Feedback Problems}
\label{sec:regulation}
A diode rectifier is deliberately used far outside its small-signal region: it
passes one polarity of an AC waveform to charge a reservoir capacitor.  A full-wave
bridge replenishes that capacitor twice per line cycle, so its ripple frequency is
\(2f_{\rm line}\) (\cref{ch:active}).  Between recharge peaks the capacitor supplies
the load, and charge balance gives the useful estimate
\[
\boxed{\quad\Delta V\approx\frac{I_{\rm load}}{f_{\rm ripple}C}.\quad}
\]
It makes the design trade visible: more capacitance, less load current, or more
frequent charging reduces ripple.  A bridge also has two forward-biased diodes in
the conducting path, so its available peak output is lower than the transformer
secondary peak by roughly two diode drops.

A voltage regulator senses output voltage, compares it with a reference, and
adjusts a pass element to reduce the error.  That is a feedback loop.  At low
frequency, large loop gain makes output changes due to load or input variation
smaller by roughly \(1+L\), exactly as in \cref{ch:feedback}.  Compensation is
needed because the pass device, output capacitor, and load add poles; an
overconfident regulator can oscillate for the same phase-margin reason as an
op-amp circuit (\cref{sec:gbw}).

\begin{exambox}
Bias, clipping, rectification, and regulation should not be collapsed into one
``diode/transistor'' fact.  Bias chooses a locally linear operating point;
rectification intentionally uses nonlinearity; regulation closes a feedback loop
around a source whose raw voltage would otherwise move.
\end{exambox}

\begin{mistakebox}
High input resistance at a FET gate does not mean every FET amplifier has high
gain, and a large BJT current gain \(\beta\) does not make its collector current
stable.  Gain and bias stability are circuit properties, set by the device \emph{and}
its surrounding impedances and feedback.
\end{mistakebox}
