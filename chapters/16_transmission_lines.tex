\chapter{Transmission Lines, Reflection, and SWR}
\label{ch:lines}
Every circuit so far has been \emph{lumped}: a finite set of components, a finite
state vector, and the assumption from \cref{ch:foundations} that the circuit is
small compared with a wavelength. A feed line breaks that assumption, and this
chapter is what replaces it. The reward is that almost nothing new is needed---the
line turns out to be built from the same \(L\) and \(C\) element laws, obeys the
same KVL and KCL, and produces the same resonances as \cref{ch:rlc,ch:rlcpar}, only
distributed along a length instead of concentrated in two parts.

The development is staged: everything through \cref{sec:stubs} treats the line as
\emph{lossless}, which is the right first model and makes every result clean, and
\cref{sec:lineloss} then puts the loss back and shows what changes---feed-line
attenuation, why it climbs with frequency, and why a mismatch measured at the
transmitter looks better than it is.

\section{Why Lines Matter}
Transmission lines are distributed systems. When a feed line is a significant
fraction of a wavelength, voltage and current vary along the line and a lumped
wire approximation fails.

\begin{controlsbox}
A transmission line is a distributed-parameter dynamical system rather than a
lumped ODE. A lumped RLC circuit has a finite state vector; a line has a
continuum of states \(v(x,t)\) and \(i(x,t)\) indexed by position \(x\). Position
becomes part of the state description, so the model is a partial differential
equation with an infinite set of natural modes rather than a handful of
eigenvalues.

This is the edge \cref{sec:infinitepoles} walked up to: order \(n\) needs no new
machinery for any finite \(n\), but the \(n\to\infty\) limit of a ladder of
identical sections is qualitatively different---it is a delay, and a delay has
infinitely many crossings of every phase condition. The transition is continuous,
which is why so much of this chapter is Part~III's algebra reused.
\end{controlsbox}

\section{The Line as a Distributed System: Telegrapher's Equations}
Model an infinitesimal slice \(\dd x\) of a lossless line as a series inductance
\(L'\,\dd x\) and a shunt capacitance \(C'\,\dd x\), where \(L'\) and \(C'\) are
inductance and capacitance per unit length. Applying KVL and KCL to the slice
gives the \emph{telegrapher's equations},
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=-L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t},
\qquad
\frac{\partial i}{\partial x}=-C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}.
\]
These are the \emph{same} two element laws used throughout Part~III---the inductor's
\(v=L\,\dd i/\dd t\) and the capacitor's \(i=C\,\dd v/\dd t\)---applied to a slice
rather than to a discrete part. And notice their structure: a coupled pair of
first-order equations, one per energy store, exactly as in \cref{ch:rlc}. The only
difference is that the derivatives on the left are with respect to \emph{position}
instead of time, because the ``next'' store is the neighboring slice rather than a
separate component.

\subsection{From the Coupled Pair to the Wave Equation}
The series RLC chapter reduced its coupled pair to one second-order equation by
differentiating and substituting (\cref{ch:rlc}). Do the same here. Differentiate
the first telegrapher's equation with respect to \(x\):
\[
\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2}
=-L'\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial i}{\partial t}
=-L'\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial i}{\partial x},
\]
swapping the order of the two derivatives. Now substitute the second telegrapher's
equation, \(\partial i/\partial x=-C'\,\partial v/\partial t\):
\[
\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2}
=-L'\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl(-C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\Bigr)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2}=L'C'\,\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial t^2}.\;}
\]
This is the \emph{wave equation}. It is the distributed analogue of the boxed
second-order ODE of \cref{ch:rlc}, reached by the identical manoeuvre.

Any function of the combination \(t\mp x/v_p\) satisfies it, provided
\[
v_p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}},
\]
which is quickest to check by substitution: for \(v=f(t-x/v_p)\), each
\(\partial/\partial x\) contributes a factor \(-1/v_p\) and each
\(\partial/\partial t\) a factor \(1\), so the equation reduces to
\(f''/v_p^2=L'C'f''\). Solutions are therefore \emph{traveling waves}, moving in
\(+x\) or \(-x\) at speed \(v_p\) without changing shape.

\subsection{Where \texorpdfstring{\(Z_0\)}{Z0} Comes From}
The characteristic impedance is not a separate assumption; it follows from the same
substitution. Take a single forward wave \(v=f(t-x/v_p)\) and put it into the first
telegrapher's equation:
\[
\underbrace{-\frac{1}{v_p}f'}_{\partial v/\partial x}
=-L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{\partial i}{\partial t}=\frac{f'}{L'v_p}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
i=\frac{f}{L'v_p},
\]
integrating in time. The ratio of voltage to current in that wave is therefore a
constant, independent of shape and of frequency:
\[
\frac{v}{i}=L'v_p=\frac{L'}{\sqrt{L'C'}}
=\boxed{\;Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}\;}
\]
So \(Z_0\) and the velocity factor are not empirical constants---they fall out of
the per-unit-length geometry of the line. Note also what \(Z_0\) is \emph{not}: it
dissipates nothing. It is a ratio maintained by a wave in flight, which is why a
resistor equal to \(Z_0\) can absorb a wave perfectly (it presents the same ratio
the wave already has) while the lossless line itself consumes no power.

\section{Wavelength and Velocity Factor}
The \textbf{velocity factor} \(VF=v_p/c\) is the ratio of the wave's speed in the
line to the speed of light in vacuum---a dimensionless number less than one. With it,
the wavelength \emph{on the line} at frequency \(f\) is
\[
\lambda=\frac{v_p}{f}=\frac{VF\,c}{f}.
\]
Because \(v_p=1/\sqrt{L'C'}\) and a dielectric raises \(C'\), a line
with dielectric has \(VF<1\); its physical length is shorter than the free-space
electrical length. Typical values span the dielectric fill: solid-polyethylene
coax (RG-8, RG-58) runs \(VF\approx0.66\); foam-dielectric coax
\(VF\approx0.78\text{--}0.85\); PTFE coax \(VF\approx0.70\); flat twin-lead
\(VF\approx0.82\); and mostly-air open-wire or ``ladder'' line
\(VF\approx0.90\text{--}0.95\). Overhead power lines, essentially air-insulated,
are near \(VF\approx0.95\text{--}0.98\). Always use the actual line's \(VF\) when
cutting stubs or matching sections to length.

\section{Characteristic Impedance}
\(Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}\) is the voltage-to-current ratio of a traveling wave. Common
coaxial systems use \(\SI{50}{\ohm}\); \(\SI{75}{\ohm}\), \(\SI{300}{\ohm}\), and
\(\SI{450}{\ohm}\) appear in specific applications. A line terminated in \(Z_0\)
looks infinitely long---no reflection---because the load absorbs the traveling
wave exactly as more line would.

\section{Reflection Coefficient}
A wave traveling toward a load carries a fixed voltage-to-current ratio \(Z_0\). The
load demands its own ratio, \(Z_L\). If the two disagree, one wave cannot satisfy
both---so a second, backward-traveling wave appears, sized precisely to reconcile
them. That is all a reflection is, and the reconciliation is a two-line calculation.

Write the total voltage and current at the load as the sum of a forward wave
\(V^{+}\) and a backward wave \(V^{-}\). Voltages add; currents \emph{subtract},
because the backward wave carries its power the other way, so its current reverses
sign relative to its voltage:
\[
V=V^{+}+V^{-},
\qquad
I=\frac{V^{+}}{Z_0}-\frac{V^{-}}{Z_0}.
\]
The load imposes one boundary condition, \(V/I=Z_L\):
\[
Z_L=Z_0\,\frac{V^{+}+V^{-}}{V^{+}-V^{-}} .
\]
Solving for the ratio of reflected to incident amplitude defines the
\textbf{reflection coefficient} \(\Gamma=V^{-}/V^{+}\):
\[
Z_L\bigl(V^{+}-V^{-}\bigr)=Z_0\bigl(V^{+}+V^{-}\bigr)
\;\Rightarrow\;
V^{-}(Z_L+Z_0)=V^{+}(Z_L-Z_0)
\;\Rightarrow\;
\boxed{\;\Gamma=\frac{V^{-}}{V^{+}}=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}.\;}
\]
Every limiting case now follows by inspection rather than by memory. If
\(Z_L=Z_0\) the numerator vanishes, \(\Gamma=0\): the load already presents the
ratio the wave carries, so nothing reflects. If the line is \emph{shorted}
(\(Z_L=0\)) then \(\Gamma=-1\)---full reflection with the voltage inverted, since the
short forces \(V=0\). If it is \emph{open} (\(Z_L\to\infty\)) then \(\Gamma=+1\):
full reflection in phase, since the open forces \(I=0\). Both extremes give
\(|\Gamma|=1\), as they must---a lossless termination that absorbs nothing has to
return everything.

\begin{controlsbox}
This is the same conjugate-of-a-mismatch bookkeeping as \cref{ch:resistive}'s
maximum-power-transfer condition, seen dynamically. There, a source delivered the
most power when the load matched it; here, a wave transfers all of its power when the
load matches \(Z_0\). ``Matched'' means the same thing in both places: the load is
presenting the ratio the source side is built to work into.
\end{controlsbox}

\section{Standing Waves, SWR, and Return Loss}
Incident and reflected waves overlap everywhere along the line, and because they
travel in opposite directions their relative phase depends on position. At distance
\(x\) back from the load the backward wave has made a round trip of \(2x\), so
\[
|V(x)|=\bigl|V^{+}\bigr|\,\bigl|1+\Gamma\,\ee^{-\jj 2\beta x}\bigr| ,
\]
where \(\beta=2\pi/\lambda\) counts radians per unit length. The bracket sweeps
around a circle as \(x\) grows: where the two waves arrive in phase the magnitudes
\emph{add}, and where they arrive out of phase they \emph{subtract}. The pattern is
therefore stationary in space---a \textbf{standing wave}---with
\[
V_{\max}=|V^{+}|\bigl(1+|\Gamma|\bigr),
\qquad
V_{\min}=|V^{+}|\bigl(1-|\Gamma|\bigr),
\]
one quarter wavelength apart. Their ratio is the \textbf{standing-wave ratio},
and \(|V^{+}|\) cancels:
\[
\boxed{\;\mathrm{SWR}=\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}
=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}\;}
\]
SWR equals 1 for a perfect match (\(\Gamma=0\) leaves no pattern at all) and rises
with mismatch, becoming infinite for a short or open. \textbf{Return loss} (RL)
expresses the same mismatch in the decibels of \cref{sec:decibels},
\[
\mathrm{RL}=-20\log_{10}|\Gamma| ,
\]
with higher return loss meaning a \emph{smaller} reflection. Because \(\Gamma\) is
an amplitude ratio it takes the \(20\log\) form; the reflected \emph{power}
fraction is \(|\Gamma|^2\).

\begin{mistakebox}
The symbol \(\mathrm{RL}\) for return loss has nothing to do with the RL circuits of
\cref{ch:rl}; the collision is unfortunate but standard. Likewise the phase constant
\(\beta\) in this chapter is unrelated to the feedback fraction \(\beta\) of
\cref{ch:feedback}---both notations are entrenched in their own fields, and only
context distinguishes them.
\end{mistakebox}

\section{Real Lines: Attenuation and the Complex Propagation Constant}
\label{sec:lineloss}
Everything so far has assumed a \emph{lossless} line, and that assumption has been
doing real work: it is why \(|\Gamma|\) was the same everywhere, why a stub was
purely reactive, and why SWR was a property of the load alone. Real coax
dissipates, so this section puts the loss back. It costs two more per-unit-length
parameters and one more derivation, and it repays them by explaining feed-line
loss, why that loss rises with frequency, and why a mismatch looks better at the
shack than it is at the antenna.

\subsection{Two More Parameters, One More Derivation}
The lossless slice had a series inductance \(L'\dd x\) and a shunt capacitance
\(C'\dd x\). A real slice adds the two dissipative elements alongside them: a
series resistance \(R'\dd x\) for the conductors, and a shunt conductance
\(G'\dd x\) for leakage through the dielectric. Applying KVL and KCL to the slice
as before---but now in phasor form, so \(\partial/\partial t\to\jj\omega\)---gives
the telegrapher's equations with loss:
\[
\frac{\dd V}{\dd x}=-\bigl(R'+\jj\omega L'\bigr)I,
\qquad
\frac{\dd I}{\dd x}=-\bigl(G'+\jj\omega C'\bigr)V .
\]
The structure is unchanged; each element law simply picked up its lossy companion.
Now run the same manoeuvre as before---differentiate the first with respect to
\(x\) and substitute the second:
\[
\frac{\dd^2V}{\dd x^2}
=-\bigl(R'+\jj\omega L'\bigr)\frac{\dd I}{\dd x}
=\underbrace{\bigl(R'+\jj\omega L'\bigr)\bigl(G'+\jj\omega C'\bigr)}_{\gamma^2}V .
\]
So \(V(x)\) obeys \(\dd^2V/\dd x^2=\gamma^2V\), whose solutions are
\(\ee^{\mp\gamma x}\), and the constant that governs everything is the
\textbf{complex propagation constant}
\[
\boxed{\;
\gamma=\sqrt{\bigl(R'+\jj\omega L'\bigr)\bigl(G'+\jj\omega C'\bigr)}
=\alpha+\jj\beta \;}
\]
Splitting \(\gamma\) into its real and imaginary parts is what makes it useful,
because a forward wave is then
\[
V(x)=V^{+}\ee^{-\gamma x}
=V^{+}\underbrace{\ee^{-\alpha x}}_{\text{decays}}
\underbrace{\ee^{-\jj\beta x}}_{\text{rotates}} .
\]
The two parts do independent jobs: \(\beta\) is the \emph{phase constant} already
met in \cref{sec:stubs}, counting radians of delay per unit length, while
\(\alpha\) is the \textbf{attenuation constant}, shrinking the amplitude
exponentially with distance. Loss and delay, one complex number.

The same two parameters also make the characteristic impedance complex:
\[
Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R'+\jj\omega L'}{G'+\jj\omega C'}} .
\]
Setting \(R'=G'=0\) recovers everything from before---\(\gamma=\jj\omega\sqrt{L'C'}\),
so \(\alpha=0\) and \(\beta=\omega\sqrt{L'C'}\), and \(Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}\) real. The
lossless line was the special case all along.

\subsection{The Low-Loss Limit, Which Is the Practical One}
Good coax is close to lossless: \(R'\ll\omega L'\) and \(G'\ll\omega C'\) across its
useful range. Expanding \(\gamma\) to first order in the small quantities---using
\(\sqrt{1+u}\approx1+u/2\) twice---gives
\[
\gamma\approx\jj\omega\sqrt{L'C'}
\left(1+\frac{R'}{2\jj\omega L'}+\frac{G'}{2\jj\omega C'}\right),
\]
and separating real from imaginary parts leaves the two results worth remembering:
\[
\boxed{\;
\alpha\approx\frac{R'}{2Z_0}+\frac{G'Z_0}{2},
\qquad
\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{L'C'},
\qquad
Z_0\approx\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} \;}
\]
So in the low-loss regime the phase behavior and the impedance are exactly the
lossless ones---which retroactively justifies every earlier result---and the loss
appears only in \(\alpha\), as two independent contributions: a \emph{conductor}
term \(R'/2Z_0\) and a \emph{dielectric} term \(G'Z_0/2\).

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{Why loss rises with frequency, and at two different rates.} The two terms in
\(\alpha\) have different frequency dependence, and both come from physics already
met.

Conductor loss grows because of \emph{skin effect}: at higher frequency the current
crowds into a thinner outer layer of the conductor, so the usable cross-section
shrinks and \(R'\) rises roughly as \(\sqrt f\). Dielectric loss grows because the
insulation's leakage tracks the displacement current through it, making \(G'\)
roughly proportional to \(f\).

Hence coax attenuation goes as \(\sqrt f\) where conductor loss dominates---the
entire HF and VHF range for ordinary cable---and crosses over to a steeper,
proportional-to-\(f\) behavior at microwave frequencies, where the dielectric term
takes over. That is the whole explanation of the shape of a manufacturer's
attenuation-versus-frequency curve.
\end{physicalbox}

\subsection{Attenuation in Decibels}
Manufacturers do not quote \(\alpha\) in nepers per metre; they quote loss in
decibels per unit length, conventionally per \SI{100}{\foot} or per
\SI{100}{\meter}. The conversion is just \cref{sec:decibels} applied to
\(\ee^{-\alpha x}\):
\[
\text{loss in dB}=-20\log_{10}\ee^{-\alpha x}=20\,\alpha x\log_{10}\ee
\approx8.686\,\alpha x .
\]
Because it is a decibel figure it is \emph{proportional to length}, which is what
makes the per-100-foot quotation useful: double the run, double the decibels.
Representative figures for common cable, which vary between manufacturers and
should be read off the actual data sheet:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}L{0.22\textwidth}Y Y Y@{}}
\toprule
Cable & Matched loss per \SI{100}{\foot} at \SI{14}{\mega\hertz} & \dots\ and at \SI{144}{\mega\hertz} & \(Z_0\) \\
\midrule
RG-58 (small coax) & \(\approx\SI{1.5}{\decibel}\) & \(\approx\SI{5}{\decibel}\) & \SI{50}{\ohm} \\
RG-213 (large coax) & \(\approx\SI{0.6}{\decibel}\) & \(\approx\SI{2}{\decibel}\) & \SI{50}{\ohm} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{workedbox}[: does the \(\sqrt f\) law hold?]
Test the physics against the table. Between \SI{14}{\mega\hertz} and
\SI{144}{\mega\hertz} the frequency ratio is \(144/14\approx10.3\), so if conductor
loss dominates the attenuation should rise by
\[
\sqrt{10.3}\approx3.2\times .
\]
For RG-58 the table gives \(5/1.5\approx3.3\times\); for RG-213,
\(2.0/0.6\approx3.3\times\). Both match the \(\sqrt f\) prediction closely, which
confirms that ordinary coax is conductor-loss dominated through VHF---and that the
dielectric term has not yet begun to matter. Note also why open-wire line is so
much better: almost all of its field is in air, so \(G'\) is tiny, and its high
\(Z_0\) shrinks the conductor term \(R'/2Z_0\) as well.
\end{workedbox}

\subsection{Loss on a Mismatched Line}
Loss and mismatch interact, and in two directions worth separating.

\emph{Extra loss.} On a matched line a single wave travels once to the load. On a
mismatched line the reflected wave travels the length again, and the standing-wave
pattern concentrates current at some points and voltage at others---and since the
loss mechanisms are \(I^2R'\) and \(V^2G'\), those peaks dissipate more than a flat
distribution would. The total loss therefore exceeds the matched-line loss. Writing
\(a=10^{ML/10}\) for the matched-line loss \(ML\) expressed as a power ratio, the
total is
\[
\text{total loss (dB)}=10\log_{10}
\frac{a^2-|\Gamma_L|^2}{a\bigl(1-|\Gamma_L|^2\bigr)} .
\]
The formula is easier to trust after two checks. With \(|\Gamma_L|=0\) it collapses
to \(10\log_{10}(a^2/a)=ML\), the matched-line loss, as it must. And with \(a=1\) (a
\emph{lossless} line) it gives \(10\log_{10}1=0\) for \emph{any} \(|\Gamma_L|\)---so
high SWR on a lossless line costs nothing at all. That second limit is the important
one conceptually: SWR does not itself consume power. It only aggravates loss that the
line was already going to have, which is why a high SWR is a minor matter on short
runs of good cable at HF and a serious one on long runs at VHF.

\emph{Masked mismatch.} The reflected wave is also attenuated on its way back, so
the reflection coefficient seen at the input is smaller than the one at the load:
\[
\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}=\Gamma_L\,\ee^{-2\gamma\ell}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
|\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|=|\Gamma_L|\,\ee^{-2\alpha\ell} .
\]
The factor of two is the round trip, so a line with \SI{3}{\decibel} of matched-line
loss attenuates the reflected wave by \SI{6}{\decibel}---halving \(|\Gamma|\), since
\(20\log_{10}2\approx\SI{6}{\decibel}\). \Cref{ch:measurement} works that arithmetic
through and draws the practical conclusion: an SWR meter at the transmitter can
report a comfortable match while the antenna is badly mismatched.

\begin{mistakebox}
Two statements about SWR are both true and easily confused. On a \emph{lossless}
line the SWR is the same everywhere, because \(|\Gamma|\) does not change---only its
phase rotates. On a \emph{lossy} line the SWR falls as you move away from the load,
because \(|\Gamma|\) itself decays as \(\ee^{-2\alpha\ell}\). The first is the
idealization the Smith chart's constant-SWR circles assume; the second is what a
real meter reads.
\end{mistakebox}

\section{Input Impedance Along the Line}
Because the reflected wave changes phase as it travels, the impedance seen
looking into a lossless line of length \(\ell\) toward a load \(Z_L\) rotates with
position:
\[
Z_{\mathrm{in}}(\ell)=Z_0\,
\frac{Z_L+\jj Z_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0+\jj Z_L\tan\beta\ell},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.
\]
The phase constant \(\beta\) counts radians of electrical length per unit
distance. The familiar special cases are limits of this one formula:
\begin{description}
\item[Quarter-wave (\(\beta\ell=\pi/2\), \(\tan\to\infty\))]
\(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_0^2/Z_L\). The line \emph{inverts} the load impedance, which
is why \(Z_t=\sqrt{Z_0 Z_L}\) matches two real resistances.
\item[Half-wave (\(\beta\ell=\pi\), \(\tan=0\))] \(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_L\): the line
repeats the load impedance.
\item[Matched (\(Z_L=Z_0\))] \(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_0\) for any length: nothing to
rotate.
\end{description}
\section{Stubs: Line Sections as Reactances---and as Resonators}
\label{sec:stubs}
A \textbf{stub} is a short piece of line, left deliberately unterminated, used as a
circuit element rather than as a signal path. Its two useful cases are the extremes
of the formula above.

Setting \(Z_L=0\) (a \emph{shorted} stub) makes the numerator \(\jj Z_0\tan\beta\ell\)
and the denominator \(Z_0\), so
\[
Z_{\mathrm{in}}^{\text{short}}=\jj Z_0\tan\beta\ell ,
\]
and letting \(Z_L\to\infty\) (an \emph{open} stub) leaves the reciprocal behavior,
\[
Z_{\mathrm{in}}^{\text{open}}=-\jj Z_0\cot\beta\ell .
\]
Both are \emph{purely reactive}: a lossless stub cannot dissipate, so it can only
store. This makes a length of line a substitute for a capacitor or an inductor---
often a better one at VHF and above, where a real coil's parasitics dominate.

Reading the tangent tells you which. A shorted stub shorter than \(\lambda/4\) has
\(\tan\beta\ell>0\) and so looks \textbf{inductive}; an open stub shorter than
\(\lambda/4\) looks \textbf{capacitive}. Each behavior inverts every quarter
wavelength thereafter.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{A stub is the two resonators of \cref{ch:rlc,ch:rlcpar} in disguise.}
Watch what happens at the special lengths:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{quarter-wave shorted} stub has \(\beta\ell=\pi/2\), where
\(\tan\to\infty\), so \(Z_{\mathrm{in}}\to\infty\). A short at the far end appears
as an \emph{open} at the near end---exactly what the parallel tank of
\cref{ch:rlcpar} does at \(\omega_0\). Near that frequency the stub \emph{is} a
parallel resonator.
\item A \textbf{half-wave shorted} stub has \(\beta\ell=\pi\), where \(\tan=0\), so
\(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=0\): a low impedance, which is what the series resonator of
\cref{ch:rlc} presents at \(\omega_0\).
\end{itemize}
And because \(\tan\beta\ell\) blows up not once but at every
\(\beta\ell=\pi/2+n\pi\), a single piece of line is an \emph{infinite ladder} of
alternating parallel and series resonances, spaced every quarter wavelength in
electrical length---equivalently, every odd and even multiple of a base frequency.
That is the concrete meaning of the ``infinite set of natural modes'' promised in
this chapter's opening: a lumped RLC circuit has one pole pair, while a line has
infinitely many, and the tangent is how they show up in a closed-form expression.
\Cref{sec:puredelay} anticipated exactly this from the other direction: a pure
delay has unbounded phase lag at undiminished magnitude, so it must cross every
multiple of \(-180^\circ\), and a line is a delay. \Cref{sec:infinitepoles} shows
the two pictures meeting---a cascade of \(n\) identical lags becomes a pure delay
as \(n\to\infty\), which is the lumped ladder of this section taken to its limit.

This is also why a stub makes a trap. \Cref{ch:filters} built notches from lumped
LC branches; a quarter-wave shorted stub bridged across a feed line does the same
job with no components at all, presenting an open at the design frequency and a low
impedance at its even harmonics.
\end{physicalbox}

\section{Quarter-Wave and Half-Wave Transformers}
The quarter-wave limit is worth carrying one step further, because it is the
transformer formula the exam asks for. At \(\beta\ell=\pi/2\) the line inverts the
load about \(Z_0^2\),
\[
Z_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}.
\]
We want that input impedance to equal the system impedance we are feeding. Call the
\emph{section's} own characteristic impedance \(Z_t\) (it differs from the main
line's, which is the whole point) and require \(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_0\):
\[
\frac{Z_t^2}{Z_L}=Z_0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;Z_t=\sqrt{Z_0Z_L}\;}
\]
so the matching section's impedance is the \emph{geometric mean} of the two
resistances it joins. A half-wave section, by contrast, repeats the load
(\(\tan=0\)) and transforms nothing---useful for relocating a load electrically
without changing it.

Both results assume a lossless line and hold only near the design frequency, since
\(\beta\ell\) is frequency dependent---a narrowband match, as
\cref{fig:line-worked} makes quantitative.

\section{Smith Chart Interpretation}
A Smith chart is a graphical map of normalized complex impedance and reflection
coefficient. It plots the bilinear (M\"obius) transform between normalized
impedance \(z=Z/Z_0\) and \(\Gamma=(z-1)/(z+1)\), which turns resistance and
reactance grid lines into circles and arcs inside the unit \(|\Gamma|\le1\) disk.
Crucially, the \(Z_{\mathrm{in}}(\ell)\) rotation above becomes a simple rotation
about the chart's center: moving along the line spins the point on a
constant-\(|\Gamma|\) (constant-SWR) circle. For this book the point is
conceptual---the chart is not a new law of circuits but a coordinate transform
that makes the input-impedance formula something you can read off by eye.

\Cref{fig:smith-basic} shows the geometry stripped to its essentials. Three
features carry the pool's vocabulary: the horizontal line through the middle is the
\textbf{resistance axis} (where \(z\) is purely real), the circles tangent to its
right-hand end are \textbf{constant-resistance circles}, the arcs crossing them are
\textbf{constant-reactance arcs}, and the center---\(z=1\), \(\Gamma=0\)---is the
\textbf{prime center}, the perfect match. Real charts add
\textbf{wavelength scales} around the rim, since a rotation of \(180^\circ\)
corresponds to moving a quarter wavelength along the line (the same
\(\beta\ell=\pi/2\) that inverts an impedance).

\section{An Idealized Antenna on the Smith Chart}
The most useful way to connect the chart to the math is to put a real device on
it. An antenna is where the RF energy finally leaves the circuit, and near its
fundamental resonance an antenna behaves like a one-port whose impedance we can
model with ideal elements---exactly the schematic~\(\to\) model~\(\to\) plot
pattern of the earlier circuit chapters.

\subsection{Modeling the Antenna}
Near resonance, a center-fed dipole looks electrically like a \textbf{series RLC}
one-port (\cref{fig:antenna-model}): a \emph{radiation resistance} \(R_{\text{rad}}\)
in series with an inductance and capacitance that together set the resonance. The
radiation resistance is not a lossy resistor---it represents power that leaves as
radiation---but electrically it behaves like one. That means the whole of
\cref{ch:rlc} applies directly: the impedance is that chapter's series-RLC impedance,
\[
Z(\omega)=R_{\text{rad}}+\jj\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right),
\qquad
\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} ,
\]
its quality factor is that chapter's \(Q_s=\omega_0L/R\), and its half-power
bandwidth is \(BW\approx f_0/Q\).

Below \(\omega_0\) the antenna looks capacitive (\(X<0\), the ``too short''
regime); above it, inductive (\(X>0\), ``too long''); at \(\omega_0\) the
reactance vanishes and \(Z=R_{\text{rad}}\) is purely real. A half-wave dipole is
``resonant'' precisely because it is the length at which those two cancel.

\subsection{From Wavelength to Wire Length}
\label{sec:antennalength}
That last sentence has a practical corollary: the resonant length is a
\emph{wavelength} statement, so it can be turned into a number with a tape measure.
The familiar formulas are worth deriving, because doing so shows where their odd
constants come from.

In free space \(\lambda=c/f\). Working in the units the formulas are quoted
in---feet and megahertz---and using
\(c\approx\SI{9.836e8}{\foot\per\second}\),
\[
\lambda\,[\mathrm{ft}]\approx\frac{984}{f\,[\mathrm{MHz}]},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{\lambda}{2}\approx\frac{492}{f} .
\]
A real dipole is slightly \emph{shorter} than that. The wire has thickness and its
ends have capacitance to their surroundings---the \textbf{end effect}---which adds a
little capacitive loading, so resonance arrives at a physical length about
\SI{5}{\percent} short of the free-space half wavelength. Applying that factor:
\[
\boxed{\;
\ell_{\lambda/2}\,[\mathrm{ft}]\approx\frac{0.95\times492}{f}
\approx\frac{468}{f\,[\mathrm{MHz}]},
\qquad
\ell_{\lambda/4}\,[\mathrm{ft}]\approx\frac{234}{f\,[\mathrm{MHz}]} \;}
\]
the second being a quarter-wave monopole, half the dipole. So the \(468\) is not a
mysterious constant: it is \(984/2\) reduced by the end effect, and the \(234\) is
just half of it.

\begin{workedbox}[: cutting a dipole for 20 metres]
For \(f=\SI{14.1}{\mega\hertz}\), the antenna modeled above,
\[
\ell=\frac{468}{14.1}\approx\SI{33.2}{\foot}
\qquad\text{total, so about \SI{16.6}{\foot} per leg.}
\]
Compare the two \(\SI{5}{\percent}\) corrections that appear in this chapter and do
\emph{not} confuse them: the end-effect factor above applies to an antenna radiating
in space, while the velocity factor \(VF\) of \cref{sec:lineloss}'s cables applies to
a wave guided \emph{inside} a line. A \(\lambda/4\) matching \emph{stub} of RG-213 at
this frequency is \(234/14.1\times0.66\approx\SI{11}{\foot}\)---shortened by
\(VF\), not by end effect.
\end{workedbox}

\subsection{Gain, and Two Reference Antennas}
Antenna gain is a ratio, so \cref{sec:decibels} applies unchanged---but a ratio needs
a reference, and antenna work uses two:
\begin{description}
\item[dBi] gain relative to an \emph{isotropic} radiator, a hypothetical lossless
antenna radiating equally in every direction. It is a mathematical fiction, which is
what makes it a clean reference.
\item[dBd] gain relative to a half-wave \emph{dipole} in free space.
\end{description}
A dipole is not isotropic---it concentrates its radiation broadside, and that
concentration is worth \SI{2.15}{\decibel}. So the two scales differ by a constant:
\[
\boxed{\;\mathrm{dBi}=\mathrm{dBd}+2.15\;}
\]
which means a figure quoted in dBi always looks \SI{2.15}{\decibel} better than the
same antenna quoted in dBd. Nothing physical changes; only the reference does.

\begin{mistakebox}
Gain here is \emph{directivity}, not amplification. A passive antenna adds no energy:
\SI{10}{\decibel} of gain in one direction is bought by radiating less in others.
This is why gain and pattern are the same statement, and why an antenna cannot have
gain in every direction at once.
\end{mistakebox}

\subsection{Putting Numbers On It}
Take a dipole resonant in the \SI{20}{\meter} band at
\(f_0=\SI{14.1}{\mega\hertz}\), with radiation resistance
\(R_{\text{rad}}=\SI{35}{\ohm}\) and characteristic reactance
\(\omega_0L=1/(\omega_0C)=\SI{200}{\ohm}\). Then
\[
L=\frac{\SI{200}{\ohm}}{\omega_0}\approx\SI{2.26}{\micro\henry},
\qquad
C=\frac{1}{\omega_0\,(\SI{200}{\ohm})}\approx\SI{56}{\pico\farad},
\]
and---reusing \cref{ch:rlc}'s formulas unchanged---
\[
Q=\frac{\omega_0L}{R_{\text{rad}}}=\frac{200}{35}\approx5.7,
\qquad
BW\approx\frac{f_0}{Q}=\frac{\SI{14.1}{\mega\hertz}}{5.7}\approx\SI{2.5}{\mega\hertz}.
\]
That last number is the practical payoff of the whole model, and it answers a
question nothing earlier in this chapter could: \emph{what sets an antenna's usable
bandwidth?} Its \(Q\), exactly as for any other resonator. A \(Q\) near \(6\) is very
low by resonator standards---the pole pair sits far from the \(\jj\omega\) axis and
the resonance is broad---which is why a full-size dipole covers a whole amateur band.
Checking the band edges with \(\Gamma=(Z-Z_0)/(Z+Z_0)\) confirms it: at
\SI{14.0}{\mega\hertz}, \(Z\approx35-\jj2.8\ \si{\ohm}\) and
\(\mathrm{SWR}\approx1.4\); at \SI{14.35}{\mega\hertz},
\(Z\approx35+\jj7.0\ \si{\ohm}\) and \(\mathrm{SWR}\approx1.5\). The residual
\(\mathrm{SWR}\approx1.4\) even at resonance is not a reactance problem at all---it is
simply that \(35\,\si{\ohm}\ne50\,\si{\ohm}\), which is what a matching network from
\cref{ch:filters} would fix.

\begin{physicalbox}
\textbf{Why a short loaded whip is narrowband.} Shorten the antenna and
\(R_{\text{rad}}\) collapses, while the loading coil needed to cancel the now-large
capacitive reactance pushes \(\omega_0L\) \emph{up}. Both changes drive
\(Q=\omega_0L/R_{\text{rad}}\) upward, so \(BW=f_0/Q\) shrinks---which is why a mobile
whip needs retuning across a band a dipole covers in one go, and why its efficiency
suffers (more of the loss now sits in the coil's resistance than in radiation). One
formula from \cref{ch:rlc}, two practical consequences.
\end{physicalbox}

\subsection{From the Model to the Chart}
Normalizing by the system impedance, \(z(\omega)=Z(\omega)/Z_0\), the reflection
coefficient is the same M\"obius map as before,
\[
\Gamma(\omega)=\frac{z(\omega)-1}{z(\omega)+1}.
\]
Sweeping frequency traces a curve on the Smith chart (\cref{fig:smith-antenna}).
At resonance the point crosses the real axis at
\(z=R_{\text{rad}}/Z_0\); if \(R_{\text{rad}}\ne Z_0\) it lands left of center
(here \(35/50=0.7\)), a residual mismatch a matching network would move to the
center. Off resonance the point swings up (inductive) or down (capacitive),
tracing the arc a NanoVNA draws as it sweeps the band. Reading the chart is
reading the model: distance from center is \(|\Gamma|\) (hence SWR), the crossing
of the real axis marks \(\omega_0\), and which half of the chart the arc sits in
tells you whether to add series capacitance or inductance to tune it.

\begin{controlsbox}
\textbf{One pole pair, three pictures.} These are worth lining up, because they are
the same series-RLC model drawn in three coordinate systems.
\begin{itemize}
\item In \cref{ch:rlc} the impedance locus \(Z(f)=R+\jj X(f)\) was a \emph{vertical
straight line} at \(\Re\{Z\}=R\) (\cref{fig:rlc-phasor}), because \(R\) is fixed and
only the reactance sweeps.
\item In \cref{ch:rlcpar} the dual circuit's locus was a \emph{circle through the
origin} (\cref{fig:rlcpar-worked}), because taking the reciprocal of a straight line
that misses the origin produces a circle that passes through it.
\item Here the Smith chart applies \(\Gamma=(z-1)/(z+1)\), and that map likewise
sends straight lines to circles---which is why the antenna sweep in
\cref{fig:smith-antenna} is an \emph{arc}.
\end{itemize}
Nothing physical differs among the three; only the coordinates do. It is the same
point \cref{ch:fourviews} made about time, state space, and frequency, now made about
three ways of plotting one impedance.
\end{controlsbox}

\begin{exambox}
Almost every transmission-line question is one of four, and each has a single
governing formula. \emph{How bad is the mismatch?}
\(\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)\), then
\(\mathrm{SWR}=(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)\); for a purely resistive load this
shortcuts to the larger of \(Z_L/Z_0\) and \(Z_0/Z_L\). \emph{How long is the line
electrically?} \(\lambda=VF\,c/f\)---and use the real line's \(VF\), never
free-space wavelength. \emph{What does the line do to the impedance?} A quarter
wave inverts it about \(Z_0^2\), a half wave repeats it, a matched line leaves it
alone. \emph{What does an unterminated piece do?} It is a pure reactance: a shorted
stub under \(\lambda/4\) is inductive, an open stub under \(\lambda/4\) is
capacitive, and the behavior inverts every quarter wave.

Two traps. A perfect match is about \(Z_L=Z_0\), so no line length can fix a
mismatch---it only relocates it. And reflected \emph{power} is \(|\Gamma|^2\) while
return loss is \(-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|\), so watch whether a question is asking about
amplitude or power.
\end{exambox}

\section{Worked Example}
A \(\SI{100}{\ohm}\) load terminates a \(\SI{50}{\ohm}\) line:
\[
\Gamma=\frac{100-50}{100+50}=\frac{1}{3},
\qquad
\mathrm{SWR}=\frac{1+1/3}{1-1/3}=2,
\qquad
RL=-20\log_{10}(1/3)\approx \SI{9.54}{\decibel}.
\]
A quarter-wave section would need
\(Z_t=\sqrt{(50)(100)}=\SI{70.7}{\ohm}\) to remove the mismatch, giving
\(Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_t^2/Z_L=70.7^2/100=\SI{50}{\ohm}\).

\Cref{fig:line-worked} shows both halves of this example, and each panel makes a point
the algebra alone leaves abstract.

The left panel is the standing wave on the \emph{unmatched} line. Since
\(\Gamma=+1/3\) is real and positive, the reflected wave arrives in phase with the
incident one right at the load, so \(x=0\) is a voltage \emph{maximum} of
\(1+|\Gamma|=1.33\); a quarter wavelength back the round trip has added
\(180^\circ\) and the waves subtract, giving the minimum \(1-|\Gamma|=0.67\). Their
ratio is the SWR of \(2\) computed above---so ``SWR${}=2$'' means literally that a
voltmeter slid along this line would read twice as much in some places as in others.
Note the spacing: maxima repeat every \emph{half} wavelength, and a maximum is a
\emph{quarter} wavelength from the neighboring minimum.

The right panel sweeps frequency with the quarter-wave section in place. The match is
exact only at \(f_0\), where the section is precisely \(\lambda/4\); away from it
\(\beta\ell\) is no longer \(\pi/2\), the impedance inversion is imperfect, and SWR
climbs. This is what ``narrowband'' means quantitatively, and it is the reason a
quarter-wave transformer is a single-band device while the lumped L-network of
\cref{ch:filters}---which can be retuned---is not.
