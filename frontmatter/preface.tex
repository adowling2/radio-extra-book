\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
This book teaches the circuit theory behind the Amateur Extra exam from the
perspective of a (chemical) engineer who has an undergraduate background in
controls. The intended reader is comfortable with differential equations,
eigenvalues, and state-space systems, and has seen classical frequency-domain
ideas including poles and zeros, Bode plots, and the root locus. Such a reader
does not want to memorize exam answers; they want to \emph{see} each radio
circuit as a dynamical system: to write its differential equation, cast it in
state-space form, locate its poles, and read its Bode and root-locus plots. The
organizing idea of the book is that a circuit can be understood as a physical
object, a set of conservation laws, a differential equation, a state-space
system, a phasor/frequency response, and an energy balance---and that these
views offer complementary intuition about the same object.

This book was created for me. I am a chemical engineering professor at the
University of Notre Dame, with a research and teaching background in process
systems engineering. I recently earned my General ham radio license and am
studying for the Amateur Extra exam. Rather than memorize the circuit questions,
I wanted to \emph{understand} them---to see each radio circuit as a dynamical
system I could write down, analyze in state space, and understand in the
frequency domain. Moreover, when I teach process controls for chemical
engineering, I emphasize mathematical modeling with differential equations and
analysis of state-space models. We forgo frequency-domain analysis in favor of
other topics: nonlinear dynamical systems, optimization, and model predictive
control. Developing this book also gave me an excuse to review classical
analysis tools from control theory in the context of radio applications. I learn
best when I can connect mathematical concepts to physical systems. For many
chemical engineering applications, analyzing a dynamical system with a
sinusoidal input seems contrived and not realistic. But in AC circuits, the
frequency-domain perspective makes sense.

I drafted this book with extensive assistance from large language models. The
initial concept outline was generated with ChatGPT. I then used Claude Code to
refine, critique, expand, and format the text. I marked up many drafts on an
iPad, and worked with Claude Code to parse my handwritten comments into
significant revisions. These iterative critiques and revisions helped me
critically engage and ultimately understand the material. You can find these
versions in the git history associated with this project.

The book is in five parts. Part~I is a compact, exam-ready reference. Part~II
reviews, generically, the mathematics and control theory the rest of the book
leans on: complex numbers and phasors; first- and second-order linear systems in
time and state space; frequency response, Bode plots, poles, zeros, and the root
locus; feedback; and the extension to higher order. Part~III then builds the
simple circuits---RC, RL, and the two RLC arrangements---as instances of that
generic machinery. Part~IV shows that those simple circuits are what make the
advanced topics tractable: filters, transmission lines and antennas, active
circuits and oscillators, sampling and digital signal processing, receiver noise
and dynamic range, and measurement. Part~V is practice---an exam map, real
question-pool items reasoned through, and problems that span several chapters at
once. Laplace transforms and state-space models appear throughout as bridges to
familiar engineering language; they are not calculations the Amateur Extra exam
directly asks the reader to perform.

This book is narrowly scoped, and deliberately so: it goes deep on the circuit
theory the exam draws on, and leaves the rest of the syllabus alone. The exam
draws one question from each of fifty topic groups, and passing requires
thirty-seven correct. This book is about \emph{circuit theory}, so it addresses
the groups that turn on circuit behavior: resonance and \(Q\), time constants
and phase, impedance and admittance, filters and matching networks, amplifiers
and their efficiency, oscillators and phase-locked loops, op-amps, transmission
lines, the Smith chart and \(S\)-parameters, antenna feed-point impedance,
efficiency and patterns, sampling and digital signal processing, receiver noise
and dynamic range, and test equipment. It deliberately does \emph{not} cover
rules and regulations, operating practices, propagation, safety and RF exposure,
digital protocols and modes, or semiconductor device physics and digital
logic---all of which are examined, and none of which circuit theory illuminates.
Treat this book as the deepest available treatment of one part of the syllabus
and pair it with a conventional study guide for the rest; passing on this book
alone is not possible.

This is an independent educational study guide. It is not affiliated with,
endorsed by, or sponsored by the ARRL, NCVEC, or the FCC. Its worked practice is
anchored to the current (2024--2028) Amateur Extra question pool, but question
identifiers and wording change each cycle, so final preparation should be
checked against the official current pool and the current FCC rules.

Several references informed the book's coverage and terminology: the \emph{ARRL
Extra Class License Manual}, the NCVEC Element~4 question pool as published at
HamExam.org, and earlier study guides including Dan Romanchik's (KB6NU) and
others. These were used only as checks for topic coverage, terminology, and
formulas; their prose, questions, answer choices, and figures were not copied.
Full citations appear in the Bibliography.
