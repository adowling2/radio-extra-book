\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
This book teaches the circuit theory behind the Amateur Extra exam from the
perspective of a control engineer. The intended reader already knows differential
equations, eigenvalues, and state-space systems, and has at least a rusty
acquaintance with classical frequency-domain ideas---poles and zeros, Bode plots,
and the root locus. A chemical, mechanical, electrical, or systems engineer
returning to circuits after years away is exactly the audience. Such a reader does
not want to memorize exam answers; they want to \emph{see} each radio circuit as a
dynamical system: to write its differential equation, cast it in state-space form,
locate its poles, and read its Bode and root-locus plots. The organizing idea of
the book is that one circuit can be understood as a physical object, a set of
conservation laws, a differential equation, a state-space system, a
phasor/frequency response, and an energy balance---and that these views offer
complementary intuition about the same object.

My personal note on where this came from. I am a chemical engineering professor at
the University of Notre Dame, with a research and teaching background in process
systems engineering. I recently earned my General ham radio license
and am studying for the Amateur Extra exam. Rather than memorize the circuit
questions, I wanted to \emph{understand} them---to see each radio circuit as a
dynamical system I could write down, analyze in state space, and understand in the
frequency domain. This book is a study companion I
wished I had. It was drafted with the help of large language models (ChatGPT) and
then developed, corrected, and typeset iteratively with Claude Code, with me
steering the pedagogy and critiquing the text.

The book is in four parts. Part~I is a compact, exam-ready reference. Part~II
reviews, generically, the mathematics and control theory the rest of the book
leans on: complex numbers and phasors; first- and second-order linear systems in
time and state space; and frequency response, Bode plots, poles, zeros, and the
root locus. Part~III then builds each amateur-radio circuit up as an instance of
that generic machinery, one circuit at a time. Part~IV is practice---worked
examples and reasoning through real question-pool items. Laplace transforms and
state-space models appear throughout as bridges to familiar engineering language;
they are not calculations the Amateur Extra exam directly asks the reader to
perform.

\textbf{What this book covers, and what it does not.} The Amateur Extra exam draws
one question from each of fifty topic groups, and passing requires
thirty-seven correct. This book is about \emph{circuit theory}, so it addresses the
groups that turn on circuit behavior: resonance and \(Q\), time constants and phase,
impedance and admittance, filters and matching networks, amplifiers, oscillators,
op-amps, transmission lines, the Smith chart, antenna feed-point impedance, and test
equipment. It deliberately does \emph{not} cover rules and regulations, operating
practices, propagation, safety and RF exposure, digital protocols and modes, or
semiconductor device physics and digital logic---all of which are examined, and none
of which circuit theory illuminates. Several further circuit-adjacent topics are
thinner here than the exam demands, notably receiver performance metrics (noise
figure, dynamic range, intermodulation) and sampling and DSP. Treat this book as the
deepest available treatment of one part of the syllabus and pair it with a
conventional study guide for the rest; passing on this book alone is not possible.

This is an independent educational study guide. It is not affiliated with,
endorsed by, or sponsored by the ARRL, NCVEC, or the FCC. Its worked practice is
anchored to the current (2024--2028) Amateur Extra question pool, but question
identifiers and wording change each cycle, so final preparation should be checked
against the official current pool and the current FCC rules.

Several references informed the book's coverage and terminology: the \emph{ARRL
Extra Class License Manual}, the NCVEC Element~4 question pool as published at
HamExam.org, and earlier study guides including Dan Romanchik's (KB6NU) and
others. These were used only as checks for topic coverage, terminology, and
formulas; their prose, questions, answer choices, and figures were not copied.
Full citations appear in the Bibliography.
